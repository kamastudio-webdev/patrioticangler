The easiest way to catch bluegill with kids is a small bobber, a size 8 or 10 hook and a piece of worm, fished 2 to 3 feet deep next to a dock or weed edge. That setup works in almost any pond or lake from late spring through summer. Here's how to rig it, where to cast it and how to keep the trip fun.

## Why bluegill are perfect for kids

Bluegill live in ponds, lakes and slow rivers across most of the country, often right along the bank. They bite readily, so kids get action instead of waiting an hour for one nibble. They're small enough for a young kid to reel in, but they pull hard for their size, which makes every fish feel like a win.

The gear is simple and cheap. If you still need some, start with the [beginner fishing starter kit](/beginner-fishing-starter-kit/).

## Where to find bluegill

Bluegill like cover and shallow water. Start with these spots:

- **Docks.** Fish the shady side and along the edges. Bluegill often hang right under the boards.
- **Weed edges.** Find where the weeds meet open water and drop your bait along that line.
- **Shallow banks.** In late spring and summer, bluegill move into water 1 to 4 feet deep along the shore.
- **Fallen trees and brush.** Any wood in the water is worth a few casts.

On hot summer afternoons, if the shallows go quiet, try slightly deeper water off the same spots or the shade under docks and overhanging trees.

### Spawning beds

In late spring and early summer, male bluegill fan out round nests in shallow water, often 1 to 3 feet deep. The beds look like light-colored circles on the bottom, about the size of a dinner plate, and they're usually bunched together. Polarized sunglasses make them much easier to spot.

The males guard their beds and grab almost anything that comes close, so bites come fast. Cast just past a bed and let the bobber settle near the edge instead of splashing down in the middle. Bluegill can spawn more than once over a summer, so keep checking the shallows.

## The simple bobber rig

This rig catches bluegill nearly everywhere. Set it up in this order:

1. Tie a size 8 or 10 hook to the end of your line with a [Palomar or improved clinch knot](/palomar-knot-and-improved-clinch-knot/).
2. Pinch one or two small split shot onto the line about 6 inches above the hook.
3. Attach a small bobber 2 to 3 feet above the hook.
4. Thread a piece of nightcrawler about 1 inch long onto the hook, leaving just the tip of the point showing.

## Setting the bobber depth

Start with the bobber 2 to 3 feet above the hook. That puts the bait in front of most bluegill around docks and weed edges.

If the bobber lies flat on its side instead of standing up, the split shot is probably resting on the bottom. Slide the bobber a foot closer to the hook. If you get no bites in 10 minutes, move the bobber up or down a foot, or try a new spot. Near spawning beds, set the depth so the bait hangs just above the bottom.

## Teaching the cast with a spincast reel

A spincast reel is the easiest reel for kids. There's one button to learn, and tangles are rare.

<Product id="zebco-33-combo" note="The classic kid-friendly spincast combo. It usually comes with 10-pound line already on the reel, so you can rig it and fish the same day." />

Practice in the yard first with a bobber tied on and no hook. At the water, teach the cast in these steps:

1. Reel in until the bobber hangs about 6 inches below the rod tip.
2. Hold the rod with the reel on top, and press and hold the button with your thumb.
3. Look behind you for people, trees and power lines.
4. Bring the rod tip back to about 1 o'clock, just over your shoulder.
5. Swing the rod forward and let go of the button when the tip points at about 10 o'clock.
6. After the bobber lands, turn the reel handle once to lock the line.

For little kids, a short sidearm swing is safer than an overhead cast, and it's easier to aim. Stand beside them, not behind them, while they cast. Sunglasses and a hat help protect everyone's eyes and face from a wild cast.

## Watching the bobber and setting the hook

Give each kid one job: watch the bobber. Small taps and wiggles mean a fish is nibbling. When the bobber goes under or slides steadily away, it's time.

To set the hook, reel up any slack and lift the rod tip with a short, firm snap of about a foot. A big, wild jerk usually rips the hook out of a bluegill's small mouth and sends the rig flying back toward the kids.

If the bait keeps vanishing without a hookup, use a smaller piece of worm or drop to a size 10 hook. Once a fish is on, keep the rod tip up and reel steadily.

## Unhooking bluegill safely

Bluegill have sharp spines in the fin on their back. Here's how to handle them without getting poked:

- **Wet your hands first.** Dry hands rub off the slime coat that protects the fish.
- **Fold the fin down.** Slide your hand from the fish's head back toward its tail. That lays the back fin flat so the spines point backward. Then hold the fish gently around the belly.
- **Use pliers.** Grip the hook by the bend and back it out the way it went in. Long-nose fishing pliers keep fingers away from the hook point.
- **Cut deep hooks.** If a fish swallows the hook, cut the line close to its mouth instead of digging for the hook.

An adult should handle hooks for young kids. Older kids can learn with pliers after watching you a few times.

<Tip>Pinch the barb flat on each hook with pliers before you start. The hook comes out much easier, which means less handling for the fish and fewer stuck fingers.</Tip>

## Keep it short and fun

The first few trips set the tone, so plan for fun over fish count.

- **Keep trips short.** An hour is plenty for little kids. Leave while they're still having a good time.
- **Pack snacks and drinks.** A snack break saves a slow afternoon.
- **Let them reel.** Even if you hook the fish, hand them the rod.
- **Celebrate every fish.** A 3-inch bluegill counts. Take a picture before it goes back.
- **Hand out jobs.** Bobber watcher, worm picker and fish releaser all count.
- **Put life jackets on young kids.** A Coast Guard-approved life jacket that fits belongs on every young kid on a dock, a steep bank or anywhere near deep water.

<Tip>Bring a bucket of lake water. Kids can watch a fish swim for a minute before letting it go. Keep it quick, and keep the bucket out of the sun.</Tip>

## Catch-and-release basics

- Keep the fish in the water, or close to it, while you unhook it.
- Hold it gently. Don't squeeze it, and keep fingers out of the gills.
- Ease it back into the water headfirst instead of tossing it.

Bluegill are also good to eat if you want to keep a few. Adults need a fishing license in most states, and kids under a certain age often fish free. Check your state's size and bag limits before you keep any.
