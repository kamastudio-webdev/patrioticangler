The easiest way to catch your first salmon is to fish a river during the fall run, when the fish leave big water and push upstream to spawn. Drift a spawn sack along the bottom or cast a spinner across the current, and you're doing what most river salmon anglers do. If you'd rather fish open water, book a charter for your first trip.

Where you live decides which salmon you'll chase, so start there.

## Two worlds of salmon

### Pacific salmon on the West Coast and in Alaska

Five kinds of salmon run rivers from California up through Alaska. They hatch in fresh water, grow up in the ocean and return to their home river to spawn. Pacific salmon die after spawning, so each fish makes one trip home.

- **King (chinook):** the biggest salmon and the one most anglers dream about.
- **Silver (coho):** smaller than kings but aggressive and acrobatic. Coho hit spinners and spoons hard, which makes them a great first salmon.
- **Sockeye (red):** known for deep red meat. They feed mostly on plankton and rarely chase lures, so ask a local bait shop how people fish for them.
- **Pink (humpy):** the smallest and often the most plentiful. In Washington, big pink runs come mostly in odd-numbered years.
- **Chum (dog):** big, tough fish. The nickname is often said to come from the doglike teeth males grow at spawning time.

### Great Lakes salmon in the Midwest and New York

Chinook and coho aren't native to the Great Lakes. Michigan began stocking them in the 1960s, other states followed, and stocking still supports much of the fishing today. They grow up in the open lake, then run up rivers and creeks in late summer and fall.

Steelhead are the third big draw. A steelhead is a rainbow trout that grows up in the ocean or a Great Lake and returns to rivers to spawn. Unlike Pacific salmon, a steelhead can survive spawning and come back again. Great Lakes steelhead often start running in fall and keep coming into spring.

## Fishing the river runs

During a run, salmon move upstream in pushes, often after rain. Between pushes they rest in deep pools, slow runs and the seams below fast water. They aren't really feeding, but they'll still grab a bait or lure that drifts right past their nose.

### Drifting eggs and spawn sacks

Salmon eggs are the classic river bait, especially for Great Lakes salmon and steelhead. A spawn sack is a small cluster of cured eggs tied in fine mesh, about the size of a marble. Here's a simple drift rig:

1. Tie your main line to a small barrel swivel.
2. Tie 2 to 4 feet of slightly lighter leader, like 10-pound, to the other end.
3. Tie on a size 6 to 10 short-shank hook and hook the spawn sack through the mesh.
4. Pinch split shot above the swivel, just enough that the rig ticks bottom every few seconds instead of dragging.
5. Cast upstream at an angle and let the current carry the rig past you, reeling up slack as it drifts.
6. When the line stops or twitches, set the hook with a firm sweep downstream.

You can also hang the same bait under a float set so it rides just above the bottom. It snags less, and you can see the bite.

### Casting spinners and spoons

Coho, pinks and fresh kings will hit spinners and spoons, especially in deeper, slower water. The method is the one in [trout fishing with inline spinners](/trout-fishing-with-inline-spinners/), just bigger: cast across the current, let the lure sink, then reel just fast enough to turn the blade as it swings. Salmon hold near the bottom, so go slow and deep.

## Trolling the big lakes and the ocean

Out on the Great Lakes or the ocean, salmon roam open water, often deep. Boats catch them by trolling, pulling lures slowly behind a moving boat. Getting those lures down to the fish takes special gear:

- **Downriggers:** a heavy lead ball on a steel cable that lowers your line to an exact depth. A release clip lets go of your line when a fish hits.
- **Dipsy divers:** a disc tied in your line that dives as the boat pulls it, and can be set to swing off to the side.
- **Flashers:** a big, flat attractor that rolls and flashes a couple of feet ahead of a fly or squid-shaped lure, drawing salmon in.
- **Trolling spoons:** thin, light spoons that wobble at slow speeds.

For your first trip, hire a charter. The captain has the gear, knows where the fish have been and knows the local rules. Ask ahead what's included and whether you need to buy your license first.

## Gear for river salmon

- **Rod:** an 8-1/2 to 10-1/2 foot medium-heavy spinning rod. The length keeps line off the water and helps you steer a big fish in current.
- **Reel:** a medium spinning reel, roughly a 3000 to 4000 size, with a smooth drag.
- **Line:** 10- to 15-pound monofilament, or braid with a mono or fluorocarbon leader.
- **Extras:** long pliers, polarized sunglasses and boots or waders with good grip.

Braid doesn't stretch, so you feel the bottom and set the hook better at a distance. Join it to your leader with [the double uni knot](/double-uni-knot-braid-to-leader/).

## Etiquette on crowded rivers

When a run is in, popular rivers get packed shoulder to shoulder. People call it combat fishing. A few habits keep it friendly:

- **Watch before you step in.** Match how others are casting and drifting, and ask before you take a spot.
- **Take turns.** In tight spots, anglers fish a drift or two, then step down so the next person can go.
- **Don't cast across other people's lines.** Cast at the same angle as your neighbors.
- **Call "fish on!"** when you hook one so the people near you can reel in, and give others room when they hook up.
- **Pack out your trash and old line.**

## Check the rules every trip

Salmon rules are some of the most complicated in fishing, and they can change by river, by stretch and sometimes by the day. Expect rules like these:

- **Closures:** rivers can close, or open, on short notice depending on how many fish return.
- **Hatchery-only harvest:** on many West Coast rivers you can keep only hatchery fish, marked by a clipped adipose fin, the small fleshy fin on the back near the tail. Wild fish go back.
- **Hook rules:** some waters require barbless or single hooks.
- **Punch cards and tags:** some states require a catch record card or tag you fill out as soon as you keep a fish.

Carry your fishing license, and check your state wildlife agency's rules and that specific river's rules before every trip. For ocean salmon in federal waters, also check NOAA Fisheries.

<Tip>Fall rivers are cold and fast. Don't wade into current above your knees, wear a wading belt with waders, and put a life jacket on anyone fishing from a boat.</Tip>

## Common questions

### Which salmon is best for a beginner?

Coho and pinks. Both are plentiful in good years and hit spinners and spoons readily.

### Are river salmon good to eat?

Fish still bright silver from the lake or ocean eat best. As salmon get closer to spawning, they turn dark and their meat gets soft. Keep only what the rules allow.
