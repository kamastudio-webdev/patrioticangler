To catch your first catfish, tie on a slip-sinker rig with a circle hook and bait it with nightcrawlers or chicken liver. Cast it into a deep hole or river bend on a summer evening, set the rod in a holder and wait for it to bend. This setup catches channel catfish in rivers, lakes and ponds.

## Start with channel catfish

Channel catfish are the best beginner target. They live in most rivers and lakes, many states stock them in small community ponds, and they'll eat almost anything that smells strong. Most of the ones you'll catch weigh a few pounds, which makes for a fun fight on medium tackle.

You can tell a channel cat by its deeply forked tail. Smaller ones often have scattered dark spots on their sides.

Two other catfish are worth knowing:

- **Blue catfish** live mostly in big rivers and reservoirs and can grow very large. They also have forked tails, and they're partial to cut bait, especially shad.
- **Flathead catfish** have a wide, flat head, a lower jaw that sticks out and a tail that's squared off instead of forked. They hang around logs and brush and prefer live fish for bait.

Both bite the same rig with bigger hooks and heavier line.

## Where and when to find catfish

Catfish spend much of the day in deeper water and move around to feed, especially in low light. Good spots include:

- **River bends.** The outside of a bend usually has the deepest water.
- **Deep holes.** In rivers and small lakes, the deepest spot nearby often holds fish during the day.
- **Below dams.** Moving water and baitfish draw catfish to the area just downstream. Stay out of posted restricted zones, and watch for rising water when the dam releases.
- **Lake points.** Points of land that stick out into deeper water, and spots where creeks flow in, are good places to start.

Summer evenings and nights are prime time, since channel cats often move shallower after dark to feed. They also bite during the day, especially on cloudy days or in muddy water. If you fish after dark, bring a headlamp and check your footing on the bank while it's still light.

## The slip-sinker rig

The slip-sinker rig, also called a Carolina-style rig, is the one catfish rig every beginner should know. You need:

- An egg sinker, 1/2 ounce to 1 ounce (a 1/2-ounce egg sinker is about the size of a marble)
- A small plastic bead
- A barrel swivel
- 12 to 18 inches of line for the leader
- A circle hook

Tie it in this order:

1. Slide the egg sinker onto your main line.
2. Slide the bead on after it. The bead keeps the sinker from banging on your knot.
3. Tie the main line to one end of the barrel swivel with a [Palomar or improved clinch knot](/palomar-knot-and-improved-clinch-knot/).
4. Tie a 12- to 18-inch piece of line to the other end of the swivel.
5. Tie the circle hook to the end of that leader.
6. Bait the hook, keeping the point and the gap of the hook uncovered.

It works because when a catfish picks up the bait and swims off, the line slides through the sinker, so the fish doesn't feel much weight and is less likely to drop the bait. The swivel stops the sinker from sliding down to the hook.

Use a 1/2-ounce sinker in still water and go heavier in current, just heavy enough that the rig stays put on the bottom. In a pond or a slow river, you can skip the sinker, bead and swivel and pinch two or three large split shot onto the line about 12 to 18 inches above the hook. It doesn't slide, but it's quick to tie and works fine in light current.

## Why circle hooks work

A circle hook's point curves back toward the shank. When a catfish takes the bait and swims away, the tightening line pulls the hook to the corner of the fish's mouth, where it turns and sets itself. Fish are rarely hooked deep in the throat, which makes unhooking and releasing them much easier.

Don't jerk the rod to set the hook. When the rod tip bends over and stays bent, pick up the rod and reel steadily until you feel the fish's weight, then keep steady pressure. A hard jerk pulls the hook out before it has a chance to turn.

## Baits that work

Catfish find food by smell and taste as much as by sight, so strong-smelling baits are the rule.

- **Nightcrawlers.** Thread on one or two whole crawlers. They're clean and easy, and they catch catfish along with everything else.
- **Chicken liver.** A classic channel cat bait from the grocery store. It's soft, so run the hook through it two or three times and cast with a smooth lob instead of a snap.
- **Cut bait.** Chunks of shad or other baitfish about 1 inch across. Rules on which fish you can use for bait vary by state, so check before you cut up something you caught.
- **Stink bait and dip bait.** Prepared baits made just for catfish. Dip baits are usually fished on a ribbed plastic worm or a sponge made to hold them, so follow the package directions and bring a rag.

<Tip>Chicken liver stays on the hook better when it's cold. Keep the container on ice in a cooler, and take out only a few pieces at a time.</Tip>

## Line and rod

Use 10- to 15-pound monofilament. It's strong enough for a good-sized channel cat but still casts easily, and 12-pound is a good middle choice.

## Rod holders and patience

Catfishing is a waiting game. After you cast, reel up the slack, set the rod in a holder and leave the bail closed. The line needs to stay snug for the circle hook to do its job.

A rod holder can be a metal bank spike from a tackle shop or a 2-foot piece of PVC pipe pushed into the ground at an angle. Never leave a rod lying loose on the bank, because a big catfish can drag it into the water.

Give each spot 20 to 30 minutes before you move. If nothing bites, try a different depth or a new hole.

You'll need a fishing license, and it's worth checking your state's rules on size limits, bag limits and how many rods you can fish at once.

<Tip>At night, clip a small bell or a glow stick onto your rod tip. You'll hear or see a bite even when you're not staring at the rod.</Tip>

## Handling catfish safely

Catfish have stiff, sharp spines at the front of the fin on their back and in each of the two side fins just behind the head. They can lock those spines straight out, and a poke from one is painful.

To hold a channel cat:

- Wet your hands or use a damp towel.
- Grip the fish from above, with your palm just behind its head. Let the back spine sit between two of your fingers, and wrap your thumb and fingers around the body behind the two side spines.
- For a bigger fish, support its belly with your other hand.
- Use pliers to remove the hook. With a circle hook, it's usually in the corner of the mouth and backs out easily.

## Common questions

### Can I use regular hooks instead of circle hooks?

Yes. A regular J-hook or baitholder hook works, but you'll need to set the hook with a firm sweep of the rod when you get a bite, and fish swallow J-hooks deep more often. Circle hooks are easier for beginners because the rod in the holder does most of the work.

### Do catfish bite in cold weather?

Yes, but they slow down and hold in the deepest water they can find. Late spring through early fall is the easiest time to start.
