Ice fishing comes down to safe ice, a hole and a small bait hanging right below you. Get the safety part right first, because no fish is worth falling through. After that, a hand auger, a short rod and a bucket to sit on will catch perch, bluegill, crappie, walleye and pike all winter.

## Safety first: know your ice

Common guidance from state agencies, such as the Minnesota DNR, is at least 4 inches of clear, solid ice for walking and fishing on foot. Stay off anything thinner. Snowmobiles, ATVs and vehicles need much more. Follow your own state agency's ice safety advice, and remember that no ice is ever guaranteed safe.

Thickness isn't the whole story. Ice can be thick in one spot and thin a few steps away.

- **New ice is stronger than old ice.** Clear new ice is the strongest kind. White, snowy ice is only about half as strong, and late-season ice that has thawed and refrozen can rot from the inside even when it looks thick.
- **Stay off gray ice and dark patches.** Both can mean water is soaking through or the ice is thin.
- **Avoid inlets, outlets and current.** Moving water keeps ice thin, and so do springs, narrow necks between lakes, and spots around docks and bridges.
- **Steer clear of pressure ridges.** These cracks and heaves form where sheets of ice push together, and they're often weak.
- **Watch out for snow cover.** Snow insulates the ice, slows its growth and hides weak spots.

Before you head out, stop at a local bait shop and ask about ice conditions. They hear from anglers every day. Then follow these habits:

- Go with a buddy, and tell someone where you'll be and when you'll be back.
- Wear ice picks around your neck, outside your jacket, where you can grab them fast.
- Carry a spud bar or ice chisel and strike the ice ahead of you as you walk. If it punches through easily, turn around and go back the way you came.
- Bring a throw rope and keep your phone in a waterproof bag.
- Keep kids right beside you and away from open water and holes.

## If you fall through

1. Keep your head up and get your breathing under control. Cold water makes you gasp at first, and that passes in about a minute.
2. Turn toward the direction you came from. That ice held you a moment ago.
3. Get your arms onto the ice and dig in with your picks.
4. Kick your legs hard to get your body flat, and pull yourself forward onto the ice.
5. Once you're out, don't stand up. Roll away from the hole, then crawl back along your own tracks.
6. Get warm and dry right away, and get help.

## Gear you need

- **Hand auger:** a 6-inch auger is plenty for panfish and walleye, and it drills faster by hand than a bigger one. If you're after big pike, an 8-inch hole is safer.
- **Short ice rod:** around 24 to 32 inches, with a small reel. Short rods let you sit close to the hole and feel light bites.
- **Tip-ups:** for pike and walleye. More on these below.
- **Bucket:** hauls your gear out and doubles as a seat.
- **Skimmer:** a ladle with holes for scooping slush and ice chips out of the hole. Holes skim over fast on cold days.
- **Ice cleats:** they strap over your boots. Bare ice is as slick as a rink.
- **Warm layers:** a wool or synthetic base layer, a warm middle layer, a windproof outer layer or insulated bibs, waterproof insulated boots, a warm hat and spare gloves. Skip cotton, which stays wet. Wind across open ice is what makes you cold.

## Panfish and perch: small jigs and grubs

Panfish are the easiest place to start. Use small tungsten jigs, about the size of a BB to a pea. Tungsten is heavier than lead for its size, so a tiny jig sinks fast and you can feel it on the line. Tip it with one to three waxworms, which are plump white grubs, or spikes, which are maggots. Bait shops in ice country sell both.

Drop the jig to the bottom, then reel up a few inches. Perch usually stay near the bottom, while bluegill and crappie often hold a few feet higher. Jiggle the rod tip with tiny shakes, then hold it still. Most bites come during the pause and feel like a light tap, or the tip lifts up slightly as a fish rises with the bait.

If you don't get a bite in 10 or 15 minutes, drill a new hole and move. For more on finding crappie, see [crappie fishing for beginners](/crappie-fishing-for-beginners/).

<Tip>Drill several holes before you start fishing. The noise spooks fish, so it's better to drill them all at once and then hop from hole to hole quietly.</Tip>

## Walleye: jigging spoons

Walleye bite best around dawn and dusk, near drop-offs, points and other spots where depth changes. A jigging spoon like the Swedish Pimple is a classic. Tip the hook with a minnow head or a few waxworms.

Let the spoon fall to the bottom, then reel up a few inches. Snap the rod tip up 6 to 12 inches, let the spoon flutter back down, then hold still for a few seconds. Many hits come on the fall or during the pause, so watch your line and rod tip closely. See [walleye fishing for beginners](/walleye-fishing-for-beginners/) for more on where walleye hold.

## Pike: tip-ups and big minnows

A tip-up holds a spool of line over the hole and pops up a flag when a fish takes the bait. It lets you fish a big minnow while you jig nearby. Pike like weedy bays and weed edges in fairly shallow water.

1. Spool the tip-up with tip-up line and tie on a steel or heavy fluorocarbon leader. Pike have sharp teeth that cut regular line.
2. Find the depth, then set your bait about a foot or two above the bottom or the weeds.
3. Hook a big minnow, like a sucker or a large shiner, lightly behind the back fin so it can swim.
4. Set the flag, lower the bait and set the tip-up over the hole.
5. When the flag pops up, walk over. Don't run on the ice.
6. Lift the tip-up and gently take hold of the line. When you feel the fish pulling steadily, set the hook with a firm sweep of your arm and pull the fish in hand over hand.

Use pliers or a jaw spreader to unhook pike, and keep your fingers away from their teeth. For more, see [northern pike fishing for beginners](/northern-pike-fishing-for-beginners/).

## Know the rules

Carry your fishing license, and check your state's rules before you go. Many states limit how many lines or tip-ups each angler can use, and some require you to stay within sight of them. Some require your name on tip-ups or ice shelters, or set dates when shelters must come off the ice. Size and bag limits apply in winter too.

## Common questions

### Do I need a flasher or fish finder?

No. They help you see fish and your jig below the ice, but plenty of people catch fish without one. Start simple and add electronics later.

### When is the best time to go?

Early winter, once the ice is safe, is often a great time to catch fish. Dawn and dusk are the best times of day for walleye and crappie. Never rush onto early ice before it's thick enough.
