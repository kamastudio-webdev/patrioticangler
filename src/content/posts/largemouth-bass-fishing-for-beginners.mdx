Largemouth bass live in warm lakes, ponds and slow rivers across most of the country, and they're easy to find because they love cover. Cast a soft plastic worm next to weeds, a fallen tree or a dock post, bring it back slowly, and you're already fishing the way most bass anglers do.

## How to tell it's a largemouth

A largemouth is green on top with a dark, blotchy stripe along its side. The giveaway is the mouth: with it closed, the upper jaw reaches past the back of the eye. On a [smallmouth bass](/smallmouth-bass-fishing-for-beginners/), the jaw stops around the middle of the eye, and the sides are bronze with vertical bars.

## Where largemouth live

Largemouth are native to the South and the eastern half of the country and have been stocked well beyond it. They like warm, fairly calm water and something to hide next to. They're ambush feeders, sitting tight to cover and grabbing baitfish, crawfish, frogs and small bluegill that wander by.

Start with these spots:

- **Weeds.** The outside edge where grass meets open water, plus any holes or points in a weed bed.
- **Wood.** Fallen trees, stumps, brush piles and flooded bushes.
- **Docks.** The shady side, and the posts farthest out, where the water is deepest.
- **Lily pads.** The outer edge of a pad field and any open pockets inside it.
- **Other shallow cover.** Rocks, a culvert, a riprap bank. Anything different from the bare bank around it is worth a few casts.

In a farm pond, walk the bank and cast to every piece of cover you can reach. On a big lake, start in the backs of coves and creeks, where the water is shallower and warms first.

## What bass do through the seasons

Water temperature drives most of what a bass does, so a simple thermometer is worth having.

- **Spring.** As the water warms into the upper 50s, bass move shallow to feed. In the low to mid 60s they spawn: males fan out round beds in about 1 to 6 feet of water, usually on hard bottom out of the wind, and guard them. It's the easiest time to catch bass shallow. Release bedding fish quickly, close to where you caught them.
- **Summer.** Fish shallow early and late in the day. On hot afternoons, bass slide deeper, to outside weed edges, drop-offs and points, or tuck into shade under docks and thick grass mats.
- **Fall.** As the water cools, bass follow schools of shad into the backs of creeks and coves. Moving baits like a squarebill or a soft jerkbait shine. Watch for diving birds or baitfish flicking at the surface.
- **Winter.** Bass slow down and hold deeper, often on steep banks or channel edges near their summer spots. Fish slowly on the bottom during the warmest part of the afternoon.

## Five baits to start with

You don't need a tackle box full of lures. These five, in a couple of colors each, cover shallow and deep, fast and slow.

### Texas-rigged worm

The Texas rig is the most useful bass rig to learn. The hook point tucks into the plastic, so you can drag it through weeds and brush that snag other lures. Cast it to cover, let it sink, then crawl it back along the bottom with slow lifts of the rod tip. Our [Texas rig guide](/how-to-texas-rig-a-soft-plastic-worm/) shows the rigging step by step.

### Soft jerkbait

A soft jerkbait rigged weightless looks like a hurt baitfish. Twitch the rod tip twice, pause, and repeat. It's a great choice around shallow grass in spring and when bass are chasing shad in fall. See [how to fish a Zoom Super Fluke](/how-to-fish-a-zoom-super-fluke/) for rigging and the retrieve.

### Squarebill crankbait

A squarebill is a hard plastic lure with a flat, square lip. Just reel it, and it wobbles and dives. The square lip helps it bounce off stumps, rocks and dock posts instead of wedging in, and that sudden bump often draws a strike.

Cast past shallow cover and reel at a steady, medium speed so the bait runs into it. When it hits something, pause for a half second, then keep reeling. It carries two treble hooks, so unhook fish with pliers and keep it away from kids' hands.

### Stick bait

A stick bait like the Senko looks plain: a thick worm with no tail. But it shimmies slowly as it sinks, and bass often grab it on the fall. Fish it one of two ways.

- **Weightless Texas rig.** Rig it like a Texas-rigged worm on a 3/0 or 4/0 EWG hook, but skip the weight. Cast it next to cover and let it sink on slack line.
- **Wacky rig.** Hook it straight through the middle on a 1/0 or 2/0 wacky or octopus-style hook, so both ends hang down. It falls level, with both ends wiggling. A small rubber O-ring slid onto the middle, with the hook under the ring, makes each bait last longer.

Either way, let it sink, give it a twitch or two, and let it fall again. If your line jumps or moves sideways, reel down and set the hook.

### Ned rig

A Ned rig is a small soft plastic, about 2 3/4 inches long, on a light mushroom-shaped jig head. It's small and subtle, and it shines when bass are sluggish: after a cold front, in very clear water or in winter. Let it sink to the bottom, then drag or hop it slowly. Bass of every size eat it, so it's a great way to get bites while you learn.

## Rod, reel and line

Start with a medium-power spinning rod about 6 1/2 to 7 feet long, spooled with 10- to 12-pound monofilament or fluorocarbon. It handles every bait on this list and is the easiest setup to cast without tangles. Still choosing a first reel? See [spinning vs. spincast](/spinning-vs-spincast-first-fishing-reel/).

The classic next rod is a 7-foot medium-heavy baitcaster. It has more backbone to drive a hook through a Texas-rigged worm and pull bass out of weeds and wood, and it handles heavier line, like 15-pound fluorocarbon or 30- to 50-pound braid. Baitcasters take practice to cast without backlashes, so learn on spinning gear first and keep that rod for the Ned rig and wacky rig.

## Catch and release the right way

- **Grip the lower lip.** Bass have only small, rough teeth, like sandpaper, so you can hold one by the lower jaw with your thumb inside its mouth. Watch for hooks, especially trebles.
- **Support the belly.** For anything bigger than a couple of pounds, cradle the belly with your other hand and hold the fish level.
- **Don't hang a heavy bass by its jaw.** Holding a big fish straight up and down by the lip can injure its jaw.
- **Keep it wet.** Wet your hands, unhook the fish in or near the water and keep photos quick.
- **Cut deep hooks.** If a hook is deep in the throat, cut the line close to the mouth instead of digging for it.

<Tip>Wear polarized sunglasses. They cut glare so you can see weeds, stumps and spawning beds under the surface, and they protect your eyes from a stray hook.</Tip>

Before you go, make sure your fishing license is current and check your state's bass size and bag limits, which can differ from lake to lake. Wear a life jacket in any boat or kayak.
