The double uni knot joins braided main line to a fluorocarbon or monofilament leader. You tie a uni knot with each line around the other, then pull them together so they lock. It's simple to learn, strong enough for most freshwater and inshore fishing, and you can tie it in a boat or on the bank without any tools beyond a line cutter.

## Why add a leader to braid

Braid is thin and strong for its size, casts a long way and has almost no stretch, so you feel bites and set the hook well even at the end of a long cast. But it has two weak spots:

- **It's easy to see.** Braid is opaque, and in clear water, fish can notice it near the bait.
- **It frays on hard cover.** Rocks, dock posts, shells and rough bottom can scuff and cut braid quickly.

A few feet of leader on the end fixes both problems.

- **Fluorocarbon** is harder for fish to see underwater and tougher against scrapes. It sinks, which is good for bottom baits.
- **Monofilament** costs less, ties easily and has more stretch. It floats, so it's a good match for topwater lures and baits you want to keep up high.

If you're fishing thick weeds or muddy water, where fish can't see much anyway, you can often skip the leader and tie straight to the braid.

## How long and how strong

A leader of **2 to 3 feet** covers most fishing. In very clear water, or when fish are wary, go longer, around 4 to 6 feet. A longer leader also gives you more line to work with before you have to tie a new one.

Choose the leader by the fish and the cover:

- **6- to 8-pound fluorocarbon** for smallmouth, finesse baits and clear lakes.
- **10- to 15-pound** for largemouth on spinning gear.
- **15- to 25-pound** for inshore saltwater fish around docks, shells and rocks.

In freshwater, many anglers pick a leader a little lighter than their braid, so a snag breaks the leader and costs only a few feet of line instead of a long piece of braid. Inshore, the leader is often heavier than the braid so it can stand up to shells, docks and pilings.

## How to tie the double uni knot

Practice a few times at home first. It goes much faster once your hands know the steps.

1. **Overlap the lines.** Lay the end of the braid and the end of the leader side by side, pointing in opposite directions, so they overlap by 8 to 10 inches. Pinch the middle of the overlap.
2. **Make a loop with the braid.** Take the tag end of the braid and bend it back toward the middle to form a loop that lies over both lines.
3. **Wrap the braid.** Pass the braid tag end through the loop and around both lines, wrapping about 8 times inside the loop. Keep the wraps neat and side by side.
4. **Snug the first knot.** Wet the wraps, then pull the braid tag end until the knot closes firmly around the leader. Make it snug but not fully tight, because it still needs to slide.
5. **Make a loop with the leader.** Turn to the other side and do the same thing with the leader's tag end, forming a loop over both lines.
6. **Wrap the leader.** Pass the leader tag end through its loop and around both lines about 4 to 6 times.
7. **Snug the second knot.** Wet it and pull the leader tag end until it closes.
8. **Slide the knots together.** Wet both knots, then pull the braid main line and the leader main line in opposite directions. The two knots will slide toward each other and butt together.
9. **Cinch it down.** Pull each tag end firmly once more, then pull both main lines hard to seat the knot.
10. **Trim the tags.** Cut the leader tag to about 1/8 inch. Leave the braid tag a hair longer, since braid is slick.

Then tie your hook or lure to the leader end with a [Palomar or improved clinch knot](/palomar-knot-and-improved-clinch-knot/).

<Tip>Braid can cut your fingers when you pull it tight. Wrap it a few times around a pen, a pliers handle or a gloved hand before you give the final pull.</Tip>

## How many wraps to use

Braid is thin and slick, so it needs more wraps to grip. Mono and fluorocarbon are thicker and stiffer, so they need fewer.

- **Braid:** about 8 wraps for most line. Very thin braid can take a couple more.
- **Light leader, around 6 to 12 pounds:** about 5 or 6 wraps.
- **Heavier leader, 15 pounds and up:** about 4 wraps. Stiff line won't snug down neatly with too many.

If the knot slips when you test it, add two more wraps to the braid side and tie it again.

## Test it every time

Before you cast, hold the braid in one hand and the leader in the other (or wrap each around something solid) and give a hard, steady pull. A good double uni won't budge. If it slips, looks bunched or breaks, cut it off and tie a new one. A minute here beats losing a fish and your lure.

The knot will pass through your rod guides on a cast. A neatly trimmed double uni usually goes through spinning rod guides fine, though you may hear a little tick on long casts.

<Tip>Retie the leader when it gets shorter than about 18 inches or when the last few feet feel rough. Each time you change lures, you lose a few inches.</Tip>

## The FG knot: a slimmer option

Once you're comfortable with the double uni, you may hear about the FG knot. Instead of two knots, the braid is woven around the leader in a long series of alternating wraps that grip by friction, and no knot is tied in the leader itself. The result is very slim and smooth, so it slides through rod guides with almost no bump, which is why many saltwater anglers who make long casts with heavy leaders favor it. It takes more practice to tie well, and it's easier at home than in a rocking boat, so learn the double uni first.

## Common questions

### Can I use the double uni to join other lines?

Yes. It works for braid to braid, mono to mono and mono to fluorocarbon. Use more wraps on thinner or slicker line and fewer on thicker line.

### Is the double uni strong enough for saltwater?

For most inshore fishing, yes. It's a common braid-to-leader connection for redfish, speckled trout and similar fish. For more on that kind of fishing, see [inshore saltwater fishing for beginners](/inshore-saltwater-fishing-for-beginners/).
