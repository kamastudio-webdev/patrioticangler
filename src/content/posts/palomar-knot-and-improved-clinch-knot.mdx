Learn two knots first: the Palomar and the improved clinch. Either one ties a hook, lure or swivel to your line, and between them they cover almost everything you'll do with a basic rod and reel. Use the Palomar for braid and most hooks. Use the improved clinch for monofilament, especially when the Palomar gets awkward with a bigger lure.

## Why your knot matters

Your knot is the weakest point in your line. When a fish breaks off, the line usually parts at or right next to the knot, not somewhere in the middle. A knot that's cinched dry, has crossed wraps or was pulled tight too fast can give out well below the strength printed on the spool.

The good news is that a well-tied knot holds up. Wet it, snug it slowly, trim it right and test it, and most break-offs go away.

Practice at the kitchen table before you practice on the water. If kids are learning too, have them tie onto a swivel or a hook with the point clipped off.

## How to tie a Palomar knot

The Palomar is strong, simple and hard to tie wrong. Because the line passes through the hook eye twice, it grips well, even with slick braided line.

1. **Double about 6 inches of line** to make a loop, and push the loop through the hook eye. If the eye is too small for doubled line, push the end through, then back through the eye from the other side.
2. **Tie a loose overhand knot** with the doubled line, leaving the hook hanging below it. Don't tighten it yet.
3. **Pass the hook or lure through the loop.** Pull the loop all the way over it.
4. **Wet the knot** with water or a little spit.
5. **Pull it tight slowly.** Hold the hook in one hand and pull both the main line and the tag end with the other. Guide the loop up past the eye so it doesn't catch.
6. **Trim the tag end** to about 1/8 inch, about the thickness of two stacked quarters.

<Tip>Keep the two strands side by side through the hook eye and in the overhand knot. If they twist around each other, the knot won't seat cleanly. If it looks messy when you tighten it, cut it off and tie it again.</Tip>

### When the Palomar is best

- **Braid.** It's one of the most dependable knots for slick braided line, which lets many other knots slip.
- **Hooks.** Worm hooks, panfish hooks, catfish hooks and drop-shot hooks are all easy to pass through the loop.
- **Swivels and snaps.** Small, simple, no hooks to tangle.
- **Small to medium lures** with one hook or a small treble.

Where it gets awkward: big lures with two or three treble hooks, like crankbaits and large topwater plugs. You have to pass the whole lure through the loop, and the hooks love to tangle in it. That's when the improved clinch earns its spot.

## How to tie an improved clinch knot

The improved clinch is the classic knot for monofilament and fluorocarbon. It's quick, and it works on lures with small eyes and big hooks hanging off them.

1. **Thread the end of the line through the hook eye** and pull about 6 inches through.
2. **Wrap the tag end around the main line 5 to 7 times.** An easy way is to pinch the line just above the eye and spin the hook or lure so the line twists around itself.
3. **Pass the tag end through the small loop** just above the eye, where the line first went through.
4. **Pass the tag end back through the big loop** you just made. This extra tuck is the "improved" part.
5. **Wet the knot.**
6. **Hold the tag end against the main line and pull the main line slowly.** The wraps should slide down and coil neatly against the eye, without crossing over each other.
7. **Trim the tag end** to about 1/8 inch.

### How many wraps?

Use more wraps on thin line and fewer on thick line. Heavy line is stiff, and too many wraps won't snug down evenly.

- **10-pound line and lighter:** 5 to 7 wraps.
- **12- to 17-pound line:** about 5 wraps.
- **20- to 40-pound line:** about 4 wraps.

### When to use the improved clinch

- **Monofilament and fluorocarbon line.** This is what the knot was made for.
- **Lures with small eyes**, where doubled line won't fit through.
- **Big lures with treble hooks**, where passing the whole lure through a Palomar loop is a hassle.

Skip it with braid. Braid is thin and slick, and the wraps of an improved clinch tend to slip under pressure. Tie a Palomar instead.

## Three habits for every knot

### Always wet the knot

Pulling a knot tight creates friction, and friction heats the line. That heat can weaken mono and fluorocarbon right where you need it strongest. A little water or spit lets the wraps slide into place smoothly.

### Leave a short tag

Trim the tag end to about 1/8 inch. Too long, and it catches weeds and tangles in your guides. Too short, and a knot that settles under pressure can work itself loose. Braid is slicker, so you can leave it a touch longer.

### Test it before you cast

Hold the hook steady and give the line a firm, steady pull. A good knot won't budge. If it slips, looks bunched or breaks, you just saved yourself a lost fish. Cut it off and tie a new one.

Don't test by holding a bare hook in your fingers. Grip it with pliers so the point can't slip into your hand.

## Quick recap

- **Palomar:** best for braid, hooks, swivels and small lures. Double 6 inches of line, overhand knot, pass the hook through the loop, wet, tighten, trim.
- **Improved clinch:** best for mono and fluorocarbon, small-eyed lures and big lures with trebles. 5 to 7 wraps on light line, tuck through both loops, wet, tighten, trim. Don't use it with braid.
- **Every knot:** wet it, leave a tag about 1/8 inch long, and test it with a firm pull.
