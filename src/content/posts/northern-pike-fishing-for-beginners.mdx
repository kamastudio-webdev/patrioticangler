Cast a red-and-white spoon along a weed edge, reel it back with a pause now and then, and sooner or later a pike will hammer it. Pike are aggressive and fight hard, which makes them a great fish for a beginner.

The two things that trip up new pike anglers are teeth and handling. This guide covers both, plus where to find pike each season and which lures to tie on.

## Know a pike when you see one

A northern pike has a long, narrow body, a flat mouth shaped like a duck's bill and an olive-green back with rows of light, bean-shaped spots. Pike commonly reach 2 to 3 feet long. Their mouths are full of sharp teeth, which is why you'll use a wire leader and unhook them with long pliers.

## Where pike live

Pike are a cool-water fish of the north. You'll find them in lakes, rivers and reservoirs across the Great Lakes states, the upper Midwest, the Northeast, Alaska and most of Canada.

Wherever they live, pike want weeds. They hide in vegetation and dart out to grab passing fish. Start here:

- **Weedy bays.** Shallow, sheltered bays full of vegetation, especially in spring.
- **Weed edges.** The outside edge of a weed bed, where the weeds stop and deeper water starts.
- **Points and turns in the weed line.** Anywhere the edge bends or juts out.
- **River backwaters.** Slow, weedy water next to the current, like eddies and creek mouths.

## When pike bite

### Early spring, right after ice-out

Pike spawn just as the ice goes out, in shallow weedy bays and marshes, and they stay shallow for a few weeks afterward because that water warms first. It's the easiest time to catch pike from shore. Look for shallow bays with dark, mucky bottoms, which warm fastest, and reel slowly in the cold water.

Some states close pike season in early spring to protect spawning fish, so check the dates for your water.

### Summer

As the water warms, pike spread out along weed edges. Smaller pike stay up in the weeds, while bigger ones slide deeper to cooler water along the outside edge or off points.

### Fall

As the water cools, pike feed heavily before winter and move back to weed beds that are still green. It's a good time to throw bigger lures and a favorite season for big pike. In winter, they bite through the ice; see [ice fishing for beginners](/ice-fishing-for-beginners/).

## Rod, reel and line

Use a 6-1/2 to 7-foot medium-heavy spinning rod and spool it with 14- to 20-pound monofilament, or 30- to 50-pound braid for thick weeds. Set the drag so you can pull line off by hand with a firm tug. Tie the line to your leader with a [Palomar or improved clinch knot](/palomar-knot-and-improved-clinch-knot/).

## Always use a wire leader

Pike teeth slice through fishing line, even heavy line, and a bitten-off lure stays stuck in the fish's mouth.

A wire leader is a short piece of wire with a swivel on one end and a snap on the other. Tie your line to the swivel and clip the lure to the snap. A 12-inch leader in 20- to 30-pound test covers most pike fishing.

Some anglers use 18 to 24 inches of 40- to 60-pound fluorocarbon instead, mostly in clear water. It usually holds, but a pike can still bite through it. For a beginner, wire is the safer bet.

## Lures that catch pike

### Spoons

A spoon is a curved piece of metal that wobbles as you reel. The red-and-white Dardevle has been a go-to pike lure for generations.

Cast it out, count to two or three as it sinks, then reel steadily. Every few turns of the handle, stop for a second so the spoon flutters down. Pike often hit right as it starts to fall.

<Product id="dardevle-spoon" note="Made in Dearborn, Michigan. Start with the classic red-and-white; the full 1-ounce size casts far and sinks quickly to weed edges." />

### Weedless spoons over the weeds

In thick weeds, a treble hook snags on every cast. A weedless spoon has a single hook with a wire guard that slides over stems and lily pads.

Cast it over the weeds and reel just fast enough to keep it skimming the top. When it reaches an open pocket, let it drop for a second. Set the hook hard, because the fish has to push the guard out of the way.

### Inline spinners

Cast an inline spinner and reel steadily, just fast enough to keep the blade turning. For more on spinners and line twist, see [trout fishing with inline spinners](/trout-fishing-with-inline-spinners/).

### Live or dead bait under a float

Where it's legal, a live minnow or dead baitfish under a large float works well in cold water, when pike are sluggish. Set the float so the bait hangs a foot or two above the weeds, and tie the hook to a wire leader.

Set the hook soon after the float goes under and stays under. Wait too long and pike tend to swallow the bait, which makes a fish hard to release. Many states limit which baitfish you can use and whether you can move live bait between waters, so check first.

## Landing and unhooking pike

Pike thrash, and their teeth and gill rakers are sharp. Have your tools out before you hook a fish.

<Product id="long-nose-pliers" note="At about 11-1/2 inches long, these keep your hand well away from a pike's teeth." />

<Product id="cuda-jaw-spreader" note="Holds the mouth open so you can see and reach the hook. Use it gently and open the jaws only as wide as you need." />

A big rubber-mesh net helps too. It's gentler on the fish than knotted nylon, and treble hooks tangle in it less.

1. **Net the fish and keep it in the water** while you grab your pliers.
2. **Hold it behind the head.** Grip firmly across the back of the head, just behind the gill covers, fingers on the outside of the fish.
3. **Open the mouth if needed.** Use the jaw spreader only if you can't reach the hook without it.
4. **Back the hook out.** Grip it near the bend with the pliers and turn it out the way it went in. If it's deep in the throat, cut the leader close to the hook instead of digging.
5. **Support it level for a photo,** one hand under the belly and one behind the head. Holding a big pike straight up by the jaw or gills can injure it.
6. **Let it go gently.** Hold it upright in the water until it swims off.

Keep your fingers out of the mouth and gills the whole time. Have kids stand back while you unhook, since a pike with a mouthful of trebles can flip without warning.

<Tip>Pinch the barbs flat on your treble hooks before you start. They come out of a pike's mouth much faster, and out of your hand too if a flopping fish drives one in.</Tip>

## Musky: the pike's bigger cousin

Muskellunge, or musky, share many northern waters with pike and also live in some rivers and reservoirs farther south. The markings are reversed: pike have light spots on a dark body, while musky usually have dark spots or bars on a lighter body.

Musky grow bigger than pike, and there are far fewer of them, which is why anglers call them the fish of 10,000 casts. They often follow a lure to the boat, so musky anglers sweep it in a figure-eight beside the boat before lifting it out.

Many states set much higher size limits for musky than for pike, so learn to tell them apart before you keep a fish. Your state's fishing guide usually has pictures of both.

## Rules and safety

Carry your fishing license and check your state's pike rules, including size limits, bag limits and any spring closure. Spring water is dangerously cold, so wear a life jacket in a boat, and put one on kids along the bank.

## Common questions

### Are pike good to eat?

Yes. The meat is firm, white and mild. Pike do have a row of Y-shaped bones along the back that takes some practice to cut around.

### Do I need a boat?

No. In spring, pike come shallow enough to reach from shore, docks and bridges, and a weed edge within casting range is worth a try any time.
