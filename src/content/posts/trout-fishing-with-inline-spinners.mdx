Tie on a small inline spinner, cast it across the current, and reel just fast enough to keep the blade turning. That's most of what you need to know to catch trout on a spinner. It works in creeks, rivers and stocked ponds, and it's one of the easiest lures for a beginner to fish well.

Here's how to pick the right size and color, where to cast, and how to keep your line from twisting into a mess.

## Why spinners catch trout

An inline spinner has a metal blade that spins around a wire shaft as you reel. That blade does two jobs. It flashes like a small baitfish, and it sends out a steady vibration that trout can feel through the water, even when they can't see the lure clearly.

Spinners also cover water fast. You can make a cast, bring it back and move a few steps, which makes them great for finding where trout are holding on a stream you don't know yet.

## Pick the right size

For streams and stocked trout, stick with sizes #0 to #2. That's about 1/12 to 1/6 oz.

- **Size 0 (about 1/12 oz):** small creeks, low clear water and spooky fish.
- **Size 1 (about 1/8 oz):** a good all-around size for most streams and stocked trout.
- **Size 2 (about 1/6 oz):** bigger rivers, faster current, or when you need a longer cast.

Not every brand uses numbers, so go by weight when you're comparing.

## Rod, reel and line

Use a light or ultralight spinning rod about 5 to 6-1/2 feet long. A light rod can cast a 1/12 oz spinner, and its soft tip lets you feel the blade thumping as you reel.

Pair it with a small spinning reel. Spinning reels handle light lures better than most spincast reels. If you're still deciding, see [spinning vs spincast for your first reel](/spinning-vs-spincast-first-fishing-reel/).

## How to cast and retrieve a spinner

1. **Cast across and slightly upstream.** Face the stream and aim a few feet upstream of straight across. That gives the spinner time to sink before the current pulls it along.
2. **Close the bail and let it sink.** Count to two or three in shallow riffles, and five or more in a deep pool. If you snag bottom, shorten the count on the next cast.
3. **Reel just fast enough to spin the blade.** You should feel a light, steady thump through the rod. If the thump stops, speed up a little. If the spinner rises to the surface, slow down. Keep the rod tip low, around 9 or 10 o'clock.
4. **Let the current swing it.** As you reel, the current bows your line and the spinner swings across the stream. Be ready, because many strikes come as the spinner turns at the end of the swing.
5. **Reel it all the way in.** Trout often follow a spinner and grab it right near your feet, so don't lift it out of the water early.
6. **Take a few steps and cast again.** Move a few feet up or down the stream between casts so you show the spinner to new fish.

When a trout hits, keep reeling at a steady speed and lift the rod. The fish usually hooks itself on a spinner, so there's no need for a big hook set.

## Where to cast on a stream

- **Seams:** the line where fast water meets slow water. Trout rest in the slow side and dart into the fast side to grab food. Cast past the seam and bring the spinner right along it.
- **Riffles:** shallow, bumpy stretches of fast water. Look for knee-deep pockets and the slightly darker water that marks a deeper spot.
- **Pools:** deep, slow stretches, usually just below a riffle. Let the spinner sink longer before you start reeling, and work the top of the pool, where the riffle pours in.

Also cast tight to boulders, logs and undercut banks. Trout hide behind anything that breaks the current.

In a stocked pond or lake, cast out, count it down and reel steadily. Change your count until you find the depth where the fish are.

## Silver or gold?

Keep it simple: silver blades for clear water and bright days, gold blades for stained water and cloudy days. The blade does most of the work, so don't worry much about body color when you're starting out.

<Tip>If a good-looking spot hasn't given you a bite after a dozen casts with silver, switch to gold before you move on. Swapping colors takes a minute; finding a new hole takes longer.</Tip>

## Stop line twist before it starts

Spinners are known for twisting line. If the blade or the whole lure turns as you reel, that twist works its way up your line until it curls, kinks and tangles.

- **Add a small swivel a foot or two above the spinner.** Tie your main line to one end of a small barrel swivel, tie 12 to 24 inches of the same line to the other end, and tie the spinner on that. The swivel lets the twist spin out before it reaches your reel.
- **Stick with light, limp line.** 4- to 6-pound mono is easier to straighten out than heavy, stiff line.
- **Don't reel while the drag is slipping.** Turning the handle while a fish is pulling line off the reel puts twist in fast. Pump the rod up, then reel as you lower it.

If your line is already twisted, cut off the spinner and let the line drift downstream in the current for a minute. Then reel it back in under light tension.

## Rules and safety

Carry your fishing license and check your state's trout rules before you go, including size and bag limits. Some trout waters allow only artificial lures, and some require single barbless hooks. Spinners usually come with a treble hook, so on those waters, replace it with a single hook and pinch the barb flat with pliers.

Stream rocks are slippery. Wear shoes or wading boots with good grip, and don't wade into fast water above your knees. Kids fishing near fast or deep water should wear a life jacket. Treble hooks are sharp, so unhook fish with pliers and keep little ones a few steps back when someone is casting.

## Common questions

### Do I need to add bait to a spinner?

No. Spinners are made to be fished as is. On waters limited to artificial lures, adding bait to the hook breaks the rules.

### What time of day is best?

Early morning and evening are usually good, especially on bright days. On cloudy days, trout often bite through the middle of the day too.

### Why am I catching nothing but snags?

You're probably letting the spinner sink too long or reeling too slowly. Shorten your count and reel a little faster until the blade thumps steadily without touching bottom.
