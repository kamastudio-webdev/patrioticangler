Smallmouth bass live in clear, rocky water and eat mostly crawfish and small baitfish. So the plan is simple: find rock, gravel or current, and fish a small bait slowly along the bottom on light spinning gear. A Ned rig dragged over a gravel point catches them almost anywhere they swim.

## How to tell it's a smallmouth

Smallmouth are bronze to brownish green, with dark vertical bars on their sides, which is why some anglers call them bronzebacks. With the mouth closed, the upper jaw ends around the middle of the eye. On a [largemouth](/largemouth-bass-fishing-for-beginners/), the jaw reaches past the eye, and the side has one dark horizontal stripe instead of bars.

Smallmouth fight hard for their size and often jump, so hang on.

## Where smallmouth live

Smallmouth like cooler, clearer water than largemouth, and they prefer rock to weeds. They're native to the Great Lakes region and the Ohio and Mississippi river systems, and they've been stocked in many other places. You'll find them across the North, from New England through the Great Lakes and the upper Midwest, and in the upper South, where the rivers and reservoirs of Tennessee and Kentucky are well known for them.

Look for these spots:

- **Rock piles and boulders.** Underwater rock humps and big boulders on a flat or bank.
- **Gravel points.** Points of land that slope into deeper water, especially with gravel or chunk rock.
- **Rocky banks and riprap.** Bluffs, broken rock along a bank and the riprap around dams and bridges.
- **Current breaks.** In rivers, anything that blocks the current: a boulder, a bridge piling, a log, the edge of a riffle where fast water meets slow.

### How to read a river

River smallmouth face upstream and wait in slower water right next to the current, so food washes past them. That's why current breaks hold fish. Cast upstream or across the current and let your bait tumble along the bottom past the slack spot behind the rock or log. Eddies, seams between fast and slow water and the tail end of deep pools are all worth a few casts.

## What smallmouth eat

Crawfish are at the top of the list, followed by small baitfish like shiners, shad and sculpins. That makes the color choice easy. Use browns, green pumpkin and orange or red accents to look like a crawfish, and silver, white or smoke to look like baitfish.

As for timing, smallmouth move onto shallow gravel to spawn in spring and feed hard in fall. In summer, river fish stay active in the current, while lake fish often slide deeper onto rock humps and points during the day.

## Four baits for smallmouth

### Ned rig

If you only bring one bait, bring this one. A Ned rig is a short, soft plastic on a mushroom-shaped jig head. Use a light head, about 1/10 to 1/6 ounce: lighter for shallow water and slow current, heavier for deeper water, wind or stronger current.

To rig it, push the hook point into the center of the flat end of the bait, thread it on until the bait sits snug against the head, and bring the point out through the top.

To fish it, cast out and let it sink on a semi-slack line, watching where the line enters the water. Once it's on the bottom, drag it a few inches by moving the rod tip slowly from about 9 o'clock to 10 o'clock, or give it a small hop. Pause, reel up the slack, and repeat. Slow is the whole idea. A bite feels like a tick or a sudden heaviness. Reel down and set the hook with a firm, smooth sweep, not a huge swing that could snap light line.

### Crawfish crankbait

In rivers, a small crawfish-shaped crankbait is a great way to cover water. Cast across the current or a little upstream, then reel at a steady pace so the lip digs into the gravel and knocks off rocks. That bumping, darting action looks like a crawfish scooting away, and it's often when the bite comes.

Crankbaits carry treble hooks, so unhook fish with pliers. If one hangs up on a rock, give it slack before you pull. It will often float free.

### Small tube

A tube is a hollow soft plastic with a skirt of tentacles at one end. A 3- to 4-inch tube on a 1/8- to 1/4-ounce tube jig head, which slides inside the body, looks a lot like a crawfish. Drag and hop it along rocky bottom the same way you'd fish a Ned rig. On the fall, it often spirals, which smallmouth seem to like. Green pumpkin, brown and smoke are the colors to start with.

### Inline spinner

An inline spinner is about as easy as lures get: cast it and reel. In rivers and on shallow rocky flats, cast across or slightly upstream and reel just fast enough to keep the blade turning. You'll feel a steady thump through the rod. For the basics, see our guide to [fishing inline spinners for trout](/trout-fishing-with-inline-spinners/); the method is the same, just a size or two bigger.

## Light spinning gear

A 6 1/2- to 7-foot spinning rod in medium-light or medium power is the right tool. It casts a light Ned rig easily, and the soft tip helps keep small hooks pinned on a jumping fish.

For line, you have two good choices:

- **Straight 6- to 8-pound fluorocarbon or monofilament.** The simplest setup, with no extra knot. Fluorocarbon is harder to see in clear water and sinks; mono is limper and easier for beginners to manage.
- **Light braid with a fluorocarbon leader.** Use 8- to 10-pound braid as your main line, with a few feet of 6- to 8-pound fluorocarbon leader on the end. Braid is thin, casts a long way and has no stretch, so you feel every rock and bite. The leader is less visible and stands up to rocks better. Join the two with a [double uni knot](/double-uni-knot-braid-to-leader/).

Set your drag a little lighter than you would for largemouth. Smallmouth make hard runs and jumps, and light line needs to give a bit. You should be able to pull line off the reel with a firm tug.

## Handling and safety

Hold a smallmouth by the lower lip, and support the belly of bigger fish with your other hand. They thrash, so get a firm grip before you go for the hook, and keep your fingers clear of treble hooks. Wet your hands, keep the fish close to the water and let it go quickly.

River rocks are slippery, and current is stronger than it looks. Wear shoes with good grip, wade only where you can see the bottom, and wear a life jacket when wading deeper water or fishing from a boat or kayak.

Make sure your fishing license is current and check your state's rules before you keep any bass. Size limits, bag limits and seasons for smallmouth can vary by river or lake.
