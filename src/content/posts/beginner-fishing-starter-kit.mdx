For a first freshwater trip, you need a rod and reel combo, 6- or 8-pound line, a few small hooks, split shot, a couple of bobbers, pliers and a container of worms. That kit will catch bluegill, crappie and the occasional bass in almost any pond or lake.

## Get your fishing license first

Buy a license before you buy gear. Most states require one for adults, and you can usually get it in a few minutes on your state wildlife agency's website or at a bait shop. Many states let kids under a certain age fish without one, but the age varies, so check.

While you're on the agency's site, look up the size and bag limits for the water you plan to fish.

## A rod and reel combo

Buy a combo, meaning a rod and reel sold together. The parts are matched, and many combos come with line already on the reel, so you can fish the day you buy it.

The big choice is the reel. A spincast reel has a closed cover and a push button, which makes it the easiest to learn and the best pick for young kids. A spinning reel has an open spool and a wire bail, takes a few practice casts to learn, and handles light baits and more kinds of fishing. The full comparison is in [Spinning vs. Spincast: Which Reel Should You Buy First?](/spinning-vs-spincast-first-fishing-reel/)

For teens and adults, a 6- to 7-foot rod in medium-light or medium power covers most freshwater fishing. Small kids do better with a rod around 5 feet long that they can swing without hitting the ground.

## Fishing line

For general freshwater fishing, use 6- to 8-pound monofilament. Mono is cheap, easy to tie and stretches a little, which keeps small hooks from tearing out of a fish's mouth. Use 6-pound on a spinning reel for panfish and trout, and 8-pound if bass are around or the water has a lot of weeds and brush.

If your combo came with line, fish it first. Replace it when it comes off the reel in tight coils, feels rough or has been on the reel for about a year.

Braided line and fluorocarbon can wait. Each one solves a specific problem, and plain mono handles everything a beginner will run into.

Tie your hooks on with a Palomar knot or an improved clinch knot. Both are simple and strong, and [the knot guide](/palomar-knot-and-improved-clinch-knot/) walks through each one step by step.

## A small hook assortment

Most beginners buy hooks that are too big. For bluegill, crappie and other panfish, use sizes 6 to 10.

Hook sizes run backward at first: the higher the number, the smaller the hook. A size 10 is smaller than a size 6. Past size 1, the scale switches to "aught" sizes (1/0, 2/0, 3/0), and those get bigger as the number goes up. Aught sizes are for bass and catfish.

A size 8 is a good all-around panfish hook. Hooks with a long shank (the straight part) are easier to get out of a small fish's mouth.

## Split shot

Split shot are small round sinkers you pinch onto the line to pull your bait down to the fish. Put one or two about 6 to 12 inches above the hook. The removable kind has a small tab on the back, so you can squeeze it open and move or remove it. Eagle Claw's removable split shot assortment works well because it gives you several sizes in one pack.

Pinch split shot on with pliers, not your teeth. If the package says lead, wash your hands after handling it and keep it away from little kids who put things in their mouths.

## Bobbers

A bobber, also called a float, holds your bait at a set depth and shows you when a fish bites. Go small. A bobber about 3/4 inch to 1 inch across is plenty. A big bobber takes more pull to sink, so a bluegill's light bite barely moves it.

## Pliers and a line cutter

Pliers are a safety tool. You'll use them to remove hooks, especially from fish that swallow the bait, to pinch on split shot and to flatten hook barbs for easier unhooking. A 6- to 7-inch pair keeps your fingers away from hooks and fish spines.

<Product id="kastking-cutthroat-pliers" note="Seven-inch pliers with built-in line cutters, a belt-clip sheath and a coiled lanyard so they don't end up in the lake." />

For trimming line after you tie a knot, plain nail clippers work fine. A retractable cutter like the Boomerang Snip clips to your shirt or vest so it's always within reach.

## Bait and a couple of lures

Live bait is the easiest way for a beginner to catch fish. Buy a container of nightcrawlers or red worms at a bait shop. For bluegill, pinch a nightcrawler into pieces about 1 inch long, so one container goes a long way. Keep worms in the shade or in a cooler, since a hot car will kill them.

Add two or three simple lures for when you want to cast and reel. A small inline spinner and a 2-inch curly-tail grub on a 1/16-ounce jig head both catch panfish, bass and trout. [Trout fishing with inline spinners](/trout-fishing-with-inline-spinners/) shows how to fish a spinner.

## Extras that make the day better

- **Sunscreen and a hat.** Sun reflects off the water, so you burn faster than you'd expect.
- **Polarized sunglasses.** They cut glare so you can see fish and weeds under the surface, and they protect your eyes from a hook on a bad cast.
- **A 5-gallon bucket.** It carries gear, doubles as a seat and holds water if you want a quick look at a fish before letting it go.
- **A towel.** For wiping worm dirt and fish slime off your hands.
- **A small first-aid kit.** Bandages, antiseptic wipes and tweezers handle most hook pokes and scrapes.
- **A life jacket.** Anyone in a boat should wear one. Kids and weak swimmers should wear a Coast Guard-approved life jacket that fits on docks, steep banks and anywhere near deep water.
- **Water and snacks.** Especially if kids are coming.

<Tip>The night before your first trip, tie a bobber on the line with no hook and practice casting in the yard. A few minutes of practice means less time untangling line at the lake.</Tip>

## What you can skip for now

A fish finder, a pricey reel, a giant box of lures and specialty line can all wait. Fish with the basics a few times, and you'll know what you actually want next.

Bluegill are the easiest first target. [How to catch bluegill with kids](/how-to-catch-bluegill-with-kids/) shows how to rig this kit and where to find them. When you're ready for bigger fish, [catfish fishing for beginners](/catfish-fishing-for-beginners/) builds on the same basics with heavier line and a different hook.

## Starter kit checklist

- Fishing license (and your state's size and bag limits)
- Rod and reel combo, spinning or spincast
- 6- to 8-pound monofilament line
- Hooks, sizes 6 to 10
- Removable split shot assortment
- Two or three small bobbers
- Small tackle box
- Fishing pliers
- Line cutter or nail clippers
- Nightcrawlers or red worms
- Two or three small lures
- Sunscreen and a hat
- Polarized sunglasses
- 5-gallon bucket
- Towel
- Small first-aid kit
- Life jacket for boats, docks and deep water
- Water and snacks
