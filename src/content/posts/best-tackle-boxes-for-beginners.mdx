For most beginners, the best tackle box is a hard 2-tray or 3-tray box with a solid latch. It holds everything a first-timer needs, you can see it all at a glance, and it survives being dropped on a dock or tossed in the back of a truck. As your tackle grows, a few clear utility boxes give you room to expand.

Here's how the main types compare, what size to get, and how to set one up so you can find things on the water.

## Three kinds of tackle storage

### Hard tray boxes

A tray box, also called a cantilever box, has trays that fold up and out like stairs when you open the lid. Everything is in view at once. There's an open bin in the bottom for bigger items like bags of soft plastics, a spare spool of line or pliers.

Tray boxes are simple and tough, which is why they're a good first box. The downsides are weight and a fixed layout. Once the trays are full, you can't swap in a different setup for a different trip.

### Utility box systems

A utility box is a flat, clear plastic box with dividers. They're often named by size, like 3600 or 3700. You keep several of them in a bag or a bigger box and grab the ones you need for the day: one for bobber fishing, one for spinners, one for bass plastics.

This setup is easy to change and easy to see into, and a lot of experienced anglers store their tackle this way.

### Soft tackle bags

A soft bag is a padded bag with a main compartment that holds utility boxes, plus outside pockets for tools and line. Bags are lighter than hard boxes and easier to carry on a shoulder down a trail to a pond or creek.

The trade-off is protection. A bag won't keep tackle dry if it sits in a puddle in the bottom of a boat, and zippers can corrode around salt water. If you go with a bag, keep your hooks and lures in hard utility boxes inside it.

## What size does a beginner need?

Less than you'd think. A beginner fishing for bluegill, bass and trout needs a few packs of hooks, some split shot and sinkers, a handful of bobbers, a few lures and some soft plastics. That all fits in a 2-tray box.

- **For a kid:** a small 1-tray or 2-tray box they can carry with one hand. A small box keeps them from hauling more than they can handle.
- **For an adult starting out:** a 2-tray or 3-tray box, or three or four 3700-size utility boxes.
- **For a family sharing gear:** one 3-tray box for the shared tackle, plus a small box for each kid.

If you're stuck between two sizes, go one size bigger. Tackle collections tend to grow quickly.

## Features that matter

- **A solid latch.** Brass latches and thick, solid plastic latches hold up. A flimsy latch that pops open when the box tips over will scatter hooks across the dock.
- **Removable dividers.** Dividers that slide out let you make long slots for spinners and small pockets for hooks and split shot.
- **Top access.** Small compartments in the lid let you grab a hook or a sinker without opening the whole box. That's handy on a windy day or in a small boat.
- **Rust protection.** Hooks rust quickly in a damp box. Some boxes are made with rust-inhibiting plastic or come with inserts that slow rust, but keeping the box dry matters most.
- **Drainage or a good seal.** A box that sheds water, or a utility box with a rubber gasket, keeps rain and splashes off your hooks.
- **The right size for whoever carries it.** A full 3-tray box can be a heavy load for a 6-year-old.

## How to organize your tackle box

Pick one system and stick with it, so you always know where things are.

- **By technique:** one tray or box for bobber-and-bait fishing (hooks, split shot, bobbers), one for spinners and small lures, one for bass soft plastics and their hooks.
- **By species:** one box for panfish, one for bass, one for trout. This works well if you fish different spots for different fish.

A few rules no matter which system you use:

- **Keep soft plastics in their original bags.** Some soft plastics react with other plastics and can melt into a sticky mess, either with each other or with a tray. The bag also keeps the color name with the bait, so you can buy more of what works.
- **Give hooks a dry spot.** Keep hooks in their packs or in a closed compartment, away from wet lures.
- **Dry out lures before you put them away.** Set wet lures on a towel for a few minutes. If water got into the box, leave it open overnight to air out.
- **Put the things you use most on top.** Hooks, split shot and bobbers belong in the top tray or lid, where you can reach them without digging.

<Tip>Toss a few silica gel packets, the kind that come in shoe boxes, into each tray. They soak up moisture and help keep hooks from rusting.</Tip>

If kids are helping, keep loose hooks in compartments with lids, and latch the box whenever you walk away from it. Little hands like to dig, and a hook in a finger ends a fishing trip fast.

## Common questions

### Should a beginner get a tackle bag or a tackle box?

Start with a hard box. It protects hooks and lures better, and there's less to figure out. Move to a bag full of utility boxes once you own more tackle than one box can hold.

### Can soft plastics go in a tray box?

Yes, as long as they stay in their bags. Keep them in the bottom bin in their original packaging, not loose in a tray compartment.

### How do I keep hooks from rusting?

Keep the box dry, don't put wet lures back in with dry hooks, and air everything out after a rainy day. Replace hooks that show rust, since rusty hooks are weaker and dull.
