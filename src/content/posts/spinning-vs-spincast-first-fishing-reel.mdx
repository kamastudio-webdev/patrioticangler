Buy a spincast reel for young kids and for anyone who fishes only a couple of times a year. Buy a spinning reel if you're a teen or adult who plans to keep fishing. A spinning reel takes one practice session to learn, then does more of everything.

## How each reel works

### Spincast: closed face, push button

A spincast reel sits on top of the rod. A cone-shaped cover hides the spool, and the line comes out through a small hole in the front. To cast, you press and hold the button, swing the rod forward and let go of the button. Turning the handle catches the line again so you can reel in.

### Spinning: open face, bail

A spinning reel hangs under the rod with the spool in plain view. A wire arm called the bail guides line onto the spool. To cast, you flip the bail open, hold the line against the rod with your index finger, swing and lift your finger to let the line go. Then you close the bail and reel.

## Pros and cons

### Spincast

Pros:

- The easiest reel to learn. Most kids can cast one after a few tries.
- Fewer tangles for beginners, because the button takes most of the timing out of the cast.
- Usually sold as inexpensive combos with line already on the reel.

Cons:

- Shorter casts, since the line rubs on the cover as it comes off.
- Less smooth, and the drag (the setting that lets a fish pull line off the reel) is usually weaker.
- When a tangle does happen, it's hidden inside the cover.
- Hard to cast very light lures, and doesn't work well with very light line.

### Spinning

Pros:

- More versatile. Handles 4- to 10-pound line and casts small lures, down to 1/16 ounce, easily.
- Longer, more accurate casts.
- A smoother drag for fighting bigger fish.
- Problems are easy to see and fix.

Cons:

- A small learning curve with the bail and finger. Expect a few practice casts before it clicks.
- Loose loops and tangles if the line is twisted or the spool is overfilled.
- Hanging under the rod feels odd at first.

|  | Spincast | Spinning |

| How you cast | Press and release a button | Open bail, hold line with a finger, release |

## Who should buy which

Buy a spincast if:

- You're buying for a child under about 8.
- You'll mostly fish worms under a bobber at a pond.
- You fish once or twice a year and want no learning curve at all.

Buy a spinning reel if:

- You're a teen or adult and plan to fish regularly.
- You want to cast lures for bass or trout.
- You want one setup that will still be useful in five years.

Kids around 8 to 10 can go either way. If they're coordinated and interested, start them on spinning so they don't have to switch later.

## What rod to start with

For a first setup, use a 6- to 7-foot rod in medium-light or medium power.

- **Medium-light** is best for panfish, trout and small lures, with 4- to 8-pound line.
- **Medium** is the better all-around choice if you'll also fish for bass or small catfish, with 6- to 12-pound line.

Look near the handle. Most rods have a recommended line range and lure weight range printed there, and your line should fall inside that range.

For kids under about 8, a shorter rod, around 4 1/2 to 5 1/2 feet, is easier to handle. Two-piece rods fit in a car more easily and work fine for beginners.

If you buy a spinning reel by itself, size 25 or 30 (some brands write 2500 or 3000) is the right match for a 6- to 7-foot medium-light or medium rod.

## How to fill a reel with line

Many tangles start with line that was put on the reel wrong. The same method works for spinning and spincast reels, because on both the spool stays still while a part spins around it and wraps the line.

1. Run the line through the rod guide closest to the reel. On a spincast, unscrew the front cover and thread the line through the hole in it.
2. On a spinning reel, open the bail. Tie the line to the spool with an arbor knot, trim the tag end short and close the bail. On a spincast, tie the line to the spool and screw the cover back on.
3. Lay the filler spool flat on the floor, label up, so the line comes off over the top edge.
4. Pinch the line lightly between your fingers in front of the reel to keep it snug, and reel 15 to 20 turns.
5. Check for twist. Lower the rod tip to put slack in the line between the reel and the filler spool. If the line twists into loops, flip the filler spool over and keep reeling.
6. Stop when the line is about 1/8 inch below the lip of the spool. On a spincast, unscrew the cover to check the level.

Don't put a pencil through the filler spool so it spins. That method is for baitcasting reels, and it twists line on spinning and spincast reels.

Too much line causes loops that jump off the spool on the cast. On a spincast, overfilled line also rubs the inside of the cover and tangles. Too little line cuts your casting distance. For line weight, 6-pound mono suits most spinning reels, and 8- or 10-pound suits most spincast reels.

<Tip>After a cast with a spinning reel, flip the bail closed by hand instead of turning the handle. The line goes back on the spool more neatly, which cuts down on loops and twist.</Tip>

## When to upgrade

A good starter combo will catch fish for years. Think about upgrading when:

- The reel feels gritty or rough when you turn the handle.
- The drag jerks instead of letting out line smoothly.
- You fish often enough that a lighter, smoother setup would make a difference.
- You want to cast light lures farther or feel light bites better.

A better reel gives you a smoother retrieve and a steadier drag. A better rod is lighter and lets you feel bites better. Both are real improvements, so upgrade whichever one bothers you more. If you started on spincast, the next step is usually a spinning setup.

## Common questions

### Can I put a spinning reel on a spincast rod?

Not well. Spincast rods have small guides on top, made for a reel that sits above the rod. Spinning rods have bigger guides underneath, sized for line coming off an open spool. Buy the rod that matches the reel, which is one more reason combos make sense for a first setup.

### Do spinning reels work for left-handed anglers?

Yes. Most spinning reels let you move the handle to either side. Most people hold the rod in their dominant hand and reel with the other, but use whatever feels natural.
