Crappie gather in schools around brush piles, docks, bridge pilings and standing timber. Find that cover, put a live minnow or a small jig just above it on 4- to 6-pound line, and you can catch them most of the year. Once you catch one, stay put. There are usually more.

## Black crappie and white crappie

There are two kinds, and you fish for them the same way.

- **Black crappie** have dark speckles scattered all over their sides and usually seven or eight spines in the fin on their back. They tend to like clearer water, often with weeds.
- **White crappie** are paler, with dark vertical bars, and usually have five or six back spines. They handle muddier water well.

Both have big, thin mouths, which is why some anglers call them papermouths. That thin mouth matters when you set the hook, as you'll see below.

## Where to find crappie

Crappie relate to cover, and they often hang a few feet above the bottom instead of right on it. They feed by looking up, so keep your bait at their level or a little above.

- **Brush piles.** Sunken trees and brush, some natural and some put there by anglers. Many state wildlife agencies sink fish attractors and publish maps of where they are.
- **Docks.** Deeper docks with shade, especially big marina and covered boat docks.
- **Bridge pilings.** They give shade and break the current, and baitfish gather around them.
- **Standing timber.** Flooded trees in reservoirs. Crappie hold in the branches at different depths.

## Where crappie go each season

- **Spring.** This is the best time for beginners. As the water warms toward the low 60s, crappie move shallow to spawn, often 2 to 6 feet deep and sometimes deeper in clear lakes. Look for brush, stumps, reeds and docks in coves and creek arms.
- **Summer.** After the spawn, crappie slide deeper onto brush piles, channel edges, bridge pilings and deep docks. Fish early and late, or fish straight down over deep cover.
- **Fall.** Crappie scatter and follow baitfish, often into creek arms. Keep moving until you find a school.
- **Winter.** They pack tightly onto deep brush, channel bends and bridges. Bites are slow and light, but one good spot can give you a lot of fish.

## Gear: keep it light

A light or ultralight spinning rod, about 5 1/2 to 7 feet long, with a small spinning reel is ideal. Spool it with 4- to 6-pound monofilament or fluorocarbon. Thin line lets a tiny jig sink naturally and makes light bites easier to feel. The same gear works for bluegill, so it's a good setup for [fishing with kids](/how-to-catch-bluegill-with-kids/).

## Two ways to catch crappie

### Minnow under a slip bobber

A live minnow is hard to beat, especially for beginners or when the bite is slow. A slip bobber lets you fish deep, even 10 or 15 feet down, and still cast easily. The bobber slides down to the hook when you cast, then the line slips through it until it hits a small stop you've set on the line.

Rig it in this order:

1. **Slide a bobber stop onto your line**, then a small bead.
2. **Thread on the slip bobber.**
3. **Tie on a light-wire hook**, size 2 or 4, with a [Palomar or improved clinch knot](/palomar-knot-and-improved-clinch-knot/).
4. **Pinch on a split shot** about 12 inches above the hook. Add shot until the bobber stands up and sits low in the water.
5. **Hook a minnow** up through both lips, or lightly through the back just under the top fin, without hitting the spine.
6. **Slide the bobber stop** up or down the line to set your depth. If the brush top is 10 feet down, set the minnow at about 8 or 9 feet.

### Keeping minnows alive

Minnows die fast in warm, crowded water. A few habits keep them lively:

- **Keep the water cool.** Park the bucket in the shade, never in a hot car or the sun.
- **Don't overcrowd them.** Buy only as many as you'll use in a trip.
- **Swap in lake water** a little at a time as the bucket warms. Avoid tap water, which is usually chlorinated.
- **Use a small net** to grab them, and close the lid between baits.
- **Don't dump leftovers** into the lake. Many states forbid releasing bait because it can spread disease and invasive species.

### Small jig with a soft plastic

A jig covers more water than a minnow and never needs feeding. Use a 1/32- or 1/16-ounce jig head with a 1 1/2- to 2-inch soft plastic. Thread the plastic on straight, with the hook point coming out the top of the body.

Cast past the cover and count as the jig sinks. Try a count of 5, then 10, then 15 until you get a bite, then keep using that count. Reel back slow and steady, just fast enough to keep the jig from sinking. You can also hang a jig under a bobber the same way you'd fish a minnow.

## Fishing straight down around brush

When crappie hold tight to brush, docks or bridge pilings, skip the cast. From a boat or dock, lower your jig or minnow straight down beside the cover until it's just above the brush. Then hold it still, or lift and lower it a few inches at a time.

Watch your line closely. A crappie often bites while swimming up, so the line may go slack instead of pulling down. Any tap, twitch or slack line means it's time to lift.

You'll lose a few jigs in the brush. That's part of fishing where the crappie live.

<Tip>When you catch one, note the depth. Crappie in a school usually hold at about the same level, so put the next bait right back there.</Tip>

Spider rigging is an advanced boat technique in which an angler fans out several long rods in holders across the front of the boat and slowly pushes baits through open water and over cover at set depths. It catches a lot of crappie but takes plenty of gear, and some states limit how many rods one person can fish, so learn the basics first.

## Light hook set, steady pressure

Crappie mouths tear easily. Don't jerk. When you see or feel a bite, reel up the slack and lift the rod tip smoothly, from about 9 o'clock to 11 o'clock. Then keep the rod bent and reel at a steady pace.

- **Set your drag light**, so a good fish can take line instead of tearing free.
- **Don't swing big crappie into the boat** or up onto a dock by the line. That's when the hook pulls out. Use a net, or reach down and grab the fish.
- **Keep kids clear** of swinging hooks and jigs, and put life jackets on young kids on docks and in boats.

Crappie are popular to eat, and many anglers keep a few. Make sure your fishing license is current and check your state's daily limits and any size limits for the lake you fish.
