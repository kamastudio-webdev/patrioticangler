The easiest way to start offshore fishing is to book a spot on a charter boat or a party boat. The captain supplies the boat, the safety gear, the rods, the bait and the knowledge of where the fish are. You bring sunscreen, food, water and a plan for seasickness. After a few trips, you'll know whether you want to buy your own offshore gear.

## Charter boat or party boat

- **Charter boat.** Your group hires the whole boat for a half, three-quarter or full day. Many charters carry up to six passengers, and the crew focuses on your group. It costs more, but it's the best choice for families with kids.
- **Party boat (head boat).** You buy a ticket per person and share a big boat with many other anglers. Rods, tackle and bait are usually included, and most party boats bottom fish. It's the cheapest way to try offshore fishing. Expect a crowded rail and the occasional tangled line.

When you book, ask:

- What's included: rods, bait, tackle, ice and fish cleaning
- Which fish they're targeting and how far out they'll run
- What happens if the seas are too rough to go
- Whether you need your own fishing license

In many states, a licensed for-hire boat's license covers its paying passengers, but not everywhere, so ask the captain.

## What to expect on the trip

Show up early, because boats leave on time. The ride out can take an hour or longer, and it's often bumpy. On the fishing spot, the mate rigs baits, shows you how to drop or hold the rod, and helps you fight and land fish. On a party boat, drop your line when the captain gives the signal and reel up when told, so lines don't tangle.

The crew knows which fish are in season and how big they have to be, and they'll usually measure fish before they go in the cooler. Many boats fillet your catch at the dock, sometimes for a fee.

## What to bring

- **Sun protection.** A hat, a long-sleeve sun shirt and water-resistant sunscreen. Reapply often, because sun off the water burns fast.
- **Polarized sunglasses.** They cut glare and protect your eyes from swinging hooks and sinkers.
- **Food and water.** More water than you think, plus simple food like sandwiches and crackers.
- **Shoes with soft, non-slip soles.** Closed-toe shoes are best on a deck with hooks and fish around.
- **A light rain jacket.** Mornings can be cool, and spray comes over the side on the ride out.
- **A cooler with ice** for your fish, unless the boat provides one.
- **Cash** for the tip.

Some captains are superstitious about bananas on board. Leave them at home just in case.

## Have a seasickness plan

- Talk to a pharmacist or doctor about seasickness medicine, and take it before you leave the dock. Once you feel sick, it's much harder to turn around. Ask about kids separately.
- Get a good night's sleep, eat a light breakfast, and skip greasy food and alcohol the night before.
- Stay outside in the fresh air, watch the horizon, and stand near the middle of the boat, where it rocks the least. Avoid the cabin and engine exhaust.
- If you do get sick, go to the side of the boat the wind is blowing away from, and tell the mate.

If the marine forecast looks rough, ask the captain about rescheduling.

## Tipping the mate

Mates often rely on tips. On a charter, it's customary to tip the mate, commonly 15 to 20 percent of the trip price, in cash at the end of the day. On a party boat, tip the mates who helped you, more if they did a lot of untangling and baiting.

## Fish you might catch

- **Mahi-mahi.** Bright green and gold, also called dolphin, though it's no relation to the mammal. They gather under floating weed lines and debris in warm blue water.
- **Kingfish (king mackerel).** Long, fast and full of sharp teeth, so they're fished on wire leaders.
- **Snapper.** Red, vermilion and mangrove snapper live around reefs, wrecks and rocky bottom.
- **Grouper.** Heavy fish that live tight to rocks and ledges and dive for a hole when hooked.
- **Tuna.** Blackfin in the Southeast and Gulf, and yellowfin farther offshore in many areas.

## Three ways to fish offshore

### Trolling

The boat runs at a steady speed while lures or rigged baits like ballyhoo skip and swim behind it. The crew sets the lines in rod holders and picks the speed. When a rod bends and the drag screams, the mate hands it to you, and your job is to reel. Trolling covers lots of water and is the usual way to catch mahi, kingfish and tuna.

### Bottom fishing

The boat anchors or drifts over a reef, wreck or ledge, and you drop a baited rig with a heavy sinker straight down. When it hits bottom, reel up a turn or two and wait for a bite. This is what most party boats do, and it's how you catch snapper and grouper. Learn the rigs in [bottom fishing for snapper and grouper](/bottom-fishing-for-snapper-and-grouper/).

### Chunking

The boat anchors or drifts, and the crew tosses chunks of cut fish over the side a few at a time, making a trail of food in the current. Your bait, a chunk with the hook buried in it, drifts back with the free chunks on little or no weight. Tuna follow the trail toward the boat.

## If you go on your own boat

Offshore conditions change fast, and help can be far away. Before you run offshore in your own boat:

- **Life jackets.** Carry a properly fitted life jacket for every person, and have kids wear theirs. Also carry the other safety gear the Coast Guard requires for your boat's size.
- **VHF radio.** Cell phones often lose signal offshore. A marine VHF radio reaches the Coast Guard and other boats, and channel 16 is the distress and calling channel.
- **Float plan.** Tell someone on shore where you're going, who's aboard and when you'll be back, plus when to call for help if you're late.
- **Weather and seas.** Check the marine forecast for wind and wave height, and keep an eye on the sky. Summer afternoon storms build fast. If in doubt, stay in.
- **Company.** Don't go far offshore alone in a small boat. Go with an experienced friend, or run with a buddy boat.

Federal waters have their own rules, which often differ from state rules, so check NOAA Fisheries and your state agency before every trip. Some fish need extra permits too. On the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, fishing for tuna from your own boat requires a federal HMS Angling permit, and Florida requires a free State Reef Fish Angler designation to fish for many reef fish from a private boat.

## Gear basics for bottom fishing

If you want your own rod, start with a conventional setup for bottom fishing. A conventional reel sits on top of the rod and has a revolving spool, with more cranking power and line capacity than a spinning reel. Pair it with a 6- to 7-foot medium-heavy or heavy conventional rod. To drop, put the reel in free spool, keep your thumb lightly on the spool as the sinker falls, and engage the reel when it hits bottom.

### Catching live bait with a sabiki

Many offshore fish bite live bait better than dead. A sabiki rig is a string of tiny hooks dressed with bits of fish skin. Drop it into a school of baitfish around a buoy, channel marker or reef and jig it gently. Cigar minnows, sardines and small blue runners grab the little hooks, often several at once. Put them in a live well or aerated bucket right away.

With so many sharp little hooks on one line, a sabiki tangles easily and catches fingers and clothes. Keep kids clear while one is out.

### Releasing fish from deep water

Fish pulled up from deep water often suffer barotrauma: the gas in their swim bladder expands as they rise, so they float and can't swim back down. A descending device clips to the fish's lip, rides down on a weighted line and lets the fish go at depth. Some federal waters require one while you fish for reef fish, and the snapper and grouper guide explains those rules.
