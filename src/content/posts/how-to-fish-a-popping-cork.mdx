A popping cork is a float that makes noise. Hang a shrimp 18 to 36 inches below it on fluorocarbon leader, cast it over a grass flat 2 to 5 feet deep, and give the rod a sharp, short twitch so the cork chugs. Let it sit 5 to 10 seconds, then pop it again. When the cork goes under, reel and lift.

## What a popping cork does

A regular bobber just floats. A popping cork has a cupped face or a set of beads on a wire, and when you jerk it, it throws water and makes a loud chug or clack. That sound is a lot like speckled trout and redfish feeding on shrimp at the surface, and it pulls fish in from a distance to see what's going on. When they get there, they find your shrimp hanging underneath.

Each pop also makes the shrimp hop up and then sink slowly back down. That slow fall is often when the bite comes.

There are two main styles:

- **Foam corks with a cupped top.** The concave face pushes water and makes a deep chug.
- **Wire-through corks.** A float rides on a stiff wire, often with beads above and below it. When you pop it, the beads clack and the float splashes.

Both catch fish. Pick one heavy enough to cast well with your rod.

## Gear for popping cork fishing

Use the same setup that covers most inshore fishing: a 7-foot medium spinning rod, a 2500 to 3000 size reel and 10- to 15-pound braid. A longer rod helps you pick up slack and pop the cork from a distance. The [inshore saltwater fishing for beginners](/inshore-saltwater-fishing-for-beginners/) guide walks through the full setup.

## How to rig a popping cork

1. Tie your braided main line to the top eye of the cork with a [Palomar knot](/palomar-knot-and-improved-clinch-knot/).
2. Cut a piece of 20-pound fluorocarbon leader 18 to 36 inches long. The next section shows how to pick the length.
3. Tie one end of the leader to the bottom eye of the cork.
4. Tie a 1/8- or 1/4-ounce jig head, or a 1/0 circle hook for live shrimp, to the other end.
5. Add your shrimp.

### Match the leader to the water depth

Set your leader about 1 to 2 feet shorter than the water is deep. That keeps the shrimp hanging just above the grass or bottom, where fish are looking, instead of dragging through weeds.

- **3 feet of water:** about 18 inches of leader
- **4 feet of water:** 2 to 3 feet of leader
- **5 feet of water:** about 3 feet of leader

If you keep pulling up grass, shorten the leader by 6 inches. If you're over clean sand and not getting bites, try a little longer.

<Tip>Pre-tie a few leaders at 18, 24 and 30 inches with the hook or jig head already on, coil each one and keep them in a zip-top bag. When the depth changes, you can swap leaders in a minute.</Tip>

## Baits for a popping cork

- **Live shrimp.** The top choice. Hook it crosswise through the hard horn on top of its head, avoiding the dark spot inside the head, on a 1/0 circle hook. A lively shrimp kicks every time the cork pops.
- **Scented soft shrimp.** Thread a soft shrimp onto a jig head. It holds up better than live shrimp when small pinfish keep picking baits apart.
- **Pre-rigged soft shrimp.** Some soft shrimp come with the hook and weight built in, so you tie them straight to the leader.

## How to work the cork

1. Cast past the spot you want to fish. Just before the rig lands, touch the edge of the spool with your finger to slow the line, so the leader straightens out behind the cork.
2. Reel up the slack until the line is snug, and point your rod tip low, toward the cork.
3. Pop it: snap the rod tip down about a foot with a sharp, short flick of your wrist. The cork should chug and spit a little water.
4. Reel up the slack the pop made.
5. Pause 5 to 10 seconds and watch the cork the whole time. Most bites come during the pause.
6. Pop again once or twice, then pause again. Keep that rhythm all the way back.

Don't pop nonstop. Constant noise with no pause gives fish no time to find the bait. On calm days, pop more softly. In wind and chop, pop harder so the sound carries.

## When the cork goes under

When the cork disappears, slides sideways or lies flat on the water, reel quickly until you feel the fish's weight, then lift the rod smoothly. With a circle hook, skip the big sweeping hookset. The hook sets itself as the line comes tight. With a jig head, a firm lift is enough.

Keep the rod bent and let the drag do the work. Speckled trout have soft mouths, and a hard yank or a drag set too tight can tear the hook free.

<Tip>If your shrimp keeps disappearing without the cork ever going all the way under, small pinfish are likely stealing it. Move to a new spot or switch to a soft shrimp.</Tip>

## Where to fish a popping cork

- **Grass flats in 2 to 5 feet of water.** The classic spot for speckled trout. Drift across the flat with the wind, popping as you go. When you catch one, stop and work that area, since trout often travel in schools.
- **Drop-offs.** Where a flat slopes into a deeper channel, fish hold along the edge, especially on a falling tide. Cast to the shallow side and work the cork out over the edge.
- **Oyster bars.** Cast to the up-current side and let the tide carry the cork along the edge of the bar. The cork keeps your bait above the sharp shells, so you snag less than with bait on the bottom.

Fish these spots in the first couple of hours of a moving tide. When the water goes slack, the bite usually slows down.

You'll need a saltwater fishing license, and trout and redfish have size and bag limits that vary by state and change often, so check your state's current rules before you keep any.

## Common questions

### Why does my shrimp keep tangling around the cork?

The leader is wrapping around the cork during the cast. Use a smooth, sweeping cast instead of a hard snap, and slow the line with your finger just before the rig lands. That lets the shrimp land beyond the cork with the leader straight.

### Can I fish a popping cork without shrimp?

Yes. A small paddle tail on a jig head works under a cork too. A soft plastic shrimp is the easiest swap for live bait, since it looks and moves much the same.
