The best fishing gifts for a beginner are the basics they'll use on every trip: a rod and reel combo, a small tackle box, a good pair of pliers and a few proven lures. Stick to well-known gear that fits the fishing they'll actually do. A simple gift that gets them on the water beats a gadget that sits in the garage.

Below are main gifts, stocking stuffers, a few ideas that aren't gear at all, and what to skip.

## First, figure out what kind of fishing they'll do

You don't need to know much. One or two casual questions will point you to the right gear:

- **Ponds and lakes for bluegill, crappie and bass.** This is most beginners. Freshwater spinning gear is the answer.
- **A young kid, about 8 or under.** A push-button spincast combo is easier to learn.
- **The coast, from piers, beaches or a boat.** They need saltwater gear that stands up to salt.
- **Ice fishing.** Short ice rods and safety gear, not a regular combo.

Also find out what they already own. If someone gave them a rod last year, a tackle box and pliers will be more welcome than a second rod.

<Tip>Not sure what they fish for? A tackle box with hooks, split shot, bobbers and a pair of pliers works for almost any beginner, in almost any water.</Tip>

## Main gifts

### A first rod and reel combo

A combo is a rod and reel sold together, already matched. Many come with line on the reel, so the new owner can fish the same day.

For a teen or adult, a 6- to 7-foot spinning combo in medium-light or medium power handles panfish, trout, bass and small catfish. For a young child, a spincast combo is the better pick. [Spinning vs. spincast](/spinning-vs-spincast-first-fishing-reel/) explains the difference if you're stuck.

Salt corrodes reels built for fresh water, so a saltwater angler needs a combo made for it. A 7-foot medium inshore setup covers redfish, speckled trout, flounder and plenty of pier fish. ### A tackle box, filled

A 2-tray hard box holds a beginner's hooks, sinkers, bobbers and a few lures, with room to grow. The best version of this gift is a box that's already stocked: panfish hooks in sizes 6 to 10, removable split shot, two or three small bobbers and a couple of lures. Open it, rig up, go fishing.

## Stocking stuffers

Small gifts are where you can't go wrong. Every angler loses, breaks or runs out of these.

### Pliers and a line cutter

Fishing pliers take hooks out of fish, pinch on split shot and cut line. They also keep fingers away from hooks and spines, so they're a safety tool as much as a convenience.

<Product id="boomerang-snip" note="A small line cutter on a retractable cord that clips to a shirt, vest or tackle bag, so it's always within reach when they retie." />

### Bobbers

A handful of small floats is useful and easy to wrap. Small ones, about 3/4 inch to 1 inch across, show a bluegill's light bite better than big plastic balls.

### Lure kits

A small kit of proven lures is better than a giant box of random ones. Inline spinners catch trout, panfish and bass, and small crappie jigs catch nearly every panfish that swims.

## For the ice angler

If they fish through the ice, give them safety gear first. Ice picks are two spiked handles on a cord worn around the neck. If someone falls through, the picks give them a grip to pull themselves out onto slick ice.

## Gifts that aren't gear

### A fishing license

A license is a gift they'll need anyway, but how you buy one depends on the state. Some states, including Kentucky and Vermont, sell gift certificates that the recipient redeems for a license. Others let you buy a license for someone else if you have their details, such as date of birth and mailing address. Rules vary, so check your state wildlife agency's website before you plan on it.

### A morning on the water together

For a lot of beginners, the best gift is someone to go with. Write a date on a card and tuck it in with the gear. Many state wildlife agencies run free learn-to-fish clinics, and some even lend rods for free, which makes an easy first outing.

### Clothing and safety gear

- **Polarized sunglasses.** They cut glare so you can see under the surface, and they protect eyes from a bad cast.
- **A wide-brim hat and a fishing shirt with sun protection.** Sun off the water burns faster than people expect.
- **A Coast Guard-approved life jacket that fits.** For a kid, this may be the most important gift on the list. Kids' life jackets are sized by weight, not age, so check the label. When it's on and buckled, lift it by the shoulders. It shouldn't ride up past the chin.

## What to skip

- **Huge all-in-one kits with hundreds of pieces.** Most of what's in them is for fish or techniques a beginner won't use yet. A small kit of proven lures gets used.
- **Fish finders and high-end reels.** These are great later, once they know what kind of fishing they love.
- **Specialty line.** Braid and fluorocarbon each solve specific problems. Plain monofilament is the right first line.

<Tip>Wrap a fishing gift in a 5-gallon bucket. It carries gear, doubles as a seat on the bank and holds water for a quick look at a fish before it goes back.</Tip>

## Common questions

### What's a good fishing gift for a kid?

A spincast combo like the Zebco 33, a few small bobbers and a container of worms on the first trip. Add a life jacket sized for their weight. Kids care most about catching fish, so plan that first trip for a pond with lots of bluegill.

### Should I buy a combo or a separate rod and reel?

Buy a combo for a beginner. The rod and reel are already matched, and many come with line on the reel. Picking a rod and reel separately makes more sense once they know what kind of fishing they want to do.

### Is fishing gear a good gift in winter?

Yes. It gives them time to practice casting in the yard, learn a knot or two and plan a first trip for spring. If they live near the coast or in ice country, they may be able to use it right away.
