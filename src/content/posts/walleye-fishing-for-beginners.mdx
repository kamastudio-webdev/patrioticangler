Go at dawn, dusk or after dark, find a rocky point or drop-off, and hop a jig tipped with a minnow along the bottom. That's the simplest way to catch your first walleye. They're a favorite in northern lakes and rivers, and they're one of the best-tasting fish in fresh water.

The tricky part is that walleye bites are often light. Here's when and where to look, three ways to fish, and how to tell a bite from the bottom.

## Why walleye like low light

Walleye are named for their big, glassy eyes. A reflective layer at the back of each eye lets them see well in dim light, which gives them an edge over the baitfish they hunt. It also makes them sensitive to bright sun.

So walleye usually bite best:

- At dawn, at dusk and after dark
- On cloudy days
- When wind puts a chop on the water, which anglers call a "walleye chop"
- In stained or murky water, where sunlight doesn't reach as deep

On calm, bright afternoons in clear water, walleye drop deeper and hug the bottom, so fish deeper structure or come back in the evening.

## Where to find walleye

Walleye stay near the bottom and like hard structure, especially where shallow water meets deep water.

- **Points.** Rocky fingers of land that stick out into the lake. Fish the tip and both sides.
- **Rock reefs and gravel bars.** Underwater humps where walleye move up to feed in low light.
- **Drop-offs.** The edge where a shallow flat falls into deeper water, also called a break. Walleye often hold right along it.
- **Current seams below dams.** In rivers, walleye rest in the slower water beside fast current. Spring is prime time, when they run upriver to spawn. Stay out of posted restricted zones near dams.

## Rod and line

A 6-1/2-foot medium-light spinning rod with a fast, sensitive tip is a good all-around walleye rod. Spool it with 6- or 8-pound monofilament, and tie on jigs and hooks with a [Palomar or improved clinch knot](/palomar-knot-and-improved-clinch-knot/).

## Method 1: Jig and minnow

A jig is a hook with a weighted head. Tipped with a live minnow, it's the classic walleye bait.

Use the lightest jig that still lets you feel it touch bottom:

- **1/8 ounce:** water about 10 feet deep or less, with little current
- **1/4 ounce:** about 10 to 20 feet
- **3/8 ounce:** deeper water, wind or river current

Hook a 2- to 3-inch minnow up through both lips, from the bottom lip out the top.

<Product id="northland-fireball-jig" note="A short-shank jig made for live bait, so a minnow looks natural on it. Carry 1/8, 1/4 and 3/8 ounce; chartreuse is a popular color in stained water." />

1. **Cast out, or drop straight down** if you're in a boat over the spot.
2. **Let it sink to the bottom.** Your line goes slack when the jig lands.
3. **Hop it.** Lift the rod tip about 6 inches, then lower it so the jig falls back to the bottom.
4. **Pause and repeat.** Reel up the slack, wait a second or two, and hop again, working slowly back toward you.

Most bites come as the jig falls or sits still. You'll feel a light tick, or the jig will suddenly feel heavy.

## Method 2: The Lindy rig

A Lindy rig, also called a live bait rig, drags a lively bait slowly along the bottom. You need:

- A walking sinker, 1/4 to 1/2 ounce
- A small barrel swivel
- 3 to 6 feet of 6- or 8-pound line for the leader
- A plain hook, size 4 to 8
- A minnow, leech or nightcrawler

Tie it in this order:

1. Slide the walking sinker onto your main line.
2. Tie the main line to one end of the swivel. The swivel stops the sinker from sliding down to the hook.
3. Tie the leader to the other end of the swivel. Go shorter when fish are tight to the bottom and longer when they're spooky.
4. Tie the hook to the end of the leader.
5. Bait it: a minnow through the lips, a leech once through the sucker at its tail end, or a nightcrawler once through the tip of its head.

<Product id="lindy-walking-sinker" note="Rides on its heel and slides over rocks with fewer snags, and the big line hole lets a fish take the bait without feeling much weight. Use 1/4 ounce shallow and 3/8 or 1/2 ounce deeper or in wind." />

Fish it slowly. From a boat, creep along with an electric trolling motor or drift with the wind. From shore, cast it out, sweep the rod tip sideways a couple of feet, then reel up the slack and repeat. You should feel the sinker ticking along the bottom.

When you feel a tap or extra weight, drop the rod tip toward the fish so it can swim off without feeling resistance. Give it a few seconds, a little longer with a big minnow. Then reel until the line is tight and sweep the rod to set the hook.

Lively bait catches more walleye. Keep leeches and nightcrawlers cool in a cooler, and give minnows fresh water.

Bait rules vary by state, including whether you can move live bait between waters, so check before you buy.

## Method 3: Trolling crankbaits

Trolling means pulling a lure behind a slowly moving boat, which covers lots of water and finds scattered walleye.

- **At night:** in spring and fall, walleye move onto shallow rocky flats and shorelines after dark. Troll shallow-running crankbaits slowly, or cast them along riprap from shore.
- **Along breaks:** in summer, follow a drop-off with your depth finder, keeping the boat along the same depth line.

About 1.5 to 2.5 mph is a common walleye trolling speed. Let out line until the crankbait bumps bottom now and then, then reel up a turn or two so it runs just above.

<Product id="berkley-flicker-shad" note="A slim, baitfish-shaped trolling crankbait. Bigger sizes dive deeper, so check the package for running depth." />

If you troll at night, everyone should wear a life jacket, run your navigation lights and learn the water in daylight first.

## A jigging spoon for deep walleye

When walleye hold deep, past 20 feet or so, a jigging spoon gets down fast. Drop it to the bottom, reel up a foot, then snap the rod tip up a foot or two and let the spoon flutter back down. Bites usually come on the fall, so watch for the line to stop early or twitch.

## Feeling light bites

Walleye often inhale a bait and sit still, so a bite can feel like a light tick, a little extra weight, or nothing more than your line going still.

When something feels different, slowly lift the rod tip and feel for weight. If it feels heavy or alive, give it a second, then sweep the rod up firmly to set the hook. If it's just bottom, it'll feel solid and won't move.

<Tip>Watch your line where it enters the water. A walleye bite often shows as a small twitch or sideways jump in the line before you feel anything in the rod.</Tip>

## Keeping walleye for dinner

Walleye have mild, flaky white fillets and are one of the most popular fish to eat. Many waters have size limits, including slot limits that protect fish within a certain length range, plus bag limits and some spring closures during the spawn. Carry your license and check your state's rules for the water you fish.

Walleye have sharp teeth, spines in the fin on their back and sharp-edged gill covers. Grip one from above, sliding your hand from head to tail so the back fin folds down, and remove hooks with pliers.

<Tip>Pike and walleye often share the same lakes. If something bites clean through your line, it was probably a pike; see [northern pike fishing for beginners](/northern-pike-fishing-for-beginners/).</Tip>

## Common questions

### What's the best bait for walleye?

Minnows, leeches and nightcrawlers all work. Many anglers lean on minnows in the cold water of spring and fall, and switch to leeches and nightcrawlers in summer.

### Can I catch walleye from shore?

Yes. Fish rocky points, riprap, bridges and river seams below dams in the evening and after dark, when walleye move in close.
