A Texas rig is a soft plastic worm on an offset hook, with the hook point tucked back into the plastic and a sliding bullet weight in front. Because the point is hidden, you can drag it through weeds, brush and rocks that would snag almost any other lure. That's why it's one of the first bass rigs worth learning.

## Why the Texas rig is weedless

Two things keep it from snagging. First, the hook point is buried in the worm or tucked just under its skin, so there's no bare point to catch on a branch. Second, the cone-shaped bullet weight rides nose-first and pushes cover aside as you pull the rig through.

When a bass bites, it crushes the soft plastic. A hard hook set then drives the point through the worm and into the fish's mouth.

## What you need

- **A soft plastic worm or creature bait.** A 6 1/2-inch straight-tail worm is a good all-around size.
- **An EWG or offset worm hook.** The Z-shaped bend near the eye locks the worm's head in place. EWG means extra wide gap, which leaves room for thick plastic on the hook set.
- **A bullet weight.** A cone-shaped sinker that your line slides through.
- **A bead (optional).** It sits between the weight and hook, adds a little click and protects your knot.
- **A peg (optional).** A rubber bobber stop or a bit of toothpick wedged into the weight keeps it from sliding.

### Match the hook to the bait

Too small a hook and the point can't clear the plastic. Too big and the worm bunches up. Common pairings:

- **4- to 5-inch worms and small creature baits:** 1/0 or 2/0
- **6- to 7-inch worms:** 2/0 or 3/0
- **Thick 5-inch stick baits:** 3/0 or 4/0
- **8- to 10-inch worms:** 4/0 or 5/0

If the worm looks stretched or the point won't line up where it should, try one size up or down.

## How to Texas rig a worm, step by step

1. **Slide the bullet weight onto your line** with the pointed end facing your rod tip.
2. **Add a bead** if you're using one.
3. **Tie on the hook.** A Palomar knot is strong and simple. Here's [how to tie a Palomar knot](/palomar-knot-and-improved-clinch-knot/).
4. **Push the hook point straight into the center of the worm's head** about 1/4 inch, roughly the width of a pencil eraser.
5. **Turn the point out through the side** so it pops out the bottom of the worm.
6. **Slide the worm up the hook shank** until the head covers the Z bend and sits snug against the eye.
7. **Rotate the hook** so the point faces back toward the worm's body.
8. **Lay the hook alongside the worm** and pinch the spot where the hook bend lines up. That's where the point goes in.
9. **Push the point straight through the body** at that spot. Then either skin-hook it, letting the tip slip back just under the skin on top, or leave the point buried inside the plastic.
10. **Hold the line up and look at the worm.** It should hang straight, with no hump or bow. If it's crooked, back the hook out and try again a little higher or lower.

Skin-hooking hooks fish more easily but snags a bit more. Burying the point is the most weedless but needs a harder hook set. Beginners should start with skin-hooking.

<Tip>A straight worm matters more than color. A crooked worm spins, looks wrong to the fish and twists your line. Check it after every fish, since a bite often pulls the head down the hook.</Tip>

## Pick the right weight for depth and cover

Use the lightest weight that still lets you feel the bottom. A lighter weight falls slower, looks more natural and wedges in rocks less often. Go heavier when you need to get down fast, fish in wind, or push through thick weeds.

- **1/8 to 1/4 ounce:** shallow water, roughly 10 feet or less, with scattered cover. A 1/8 ounce weight is a good pick in calm water a few feet deep.
- **3/8 to 1/2 ounce:** deeper water, windy days, or any time you lose feel for the bottom.
- **3/4 ounce to 1 1/2 ounces:** "punching" through matted grass, lily pads or thick brush.

Peg the weight in heavy cover so the weight and worm go through together. In open water, leave it loose so the worm falls more naturally.

Bullet weights come in lead and tungsten, which is smaller for the same weight and gives more feel. A few states restrict small lead sinkers, so check your state's regulations, including bass size and bag limits, and make sure your fishing license is current.

## What line to use

In open water, 12- to 15-pound monofilament or fluorocarbon works fine. Around thick weeds, wood and docks, braid is better. It barely stretches, so your hook set reaches the fish, and it slices through grass.

## How to fish a Texas rig

The Texas rig is a bottom bait. Fish it slowly.

1. **Cast past your target**, such as a stump, weed edge or dock post, so the worm comes back right by it.
2. **Let it sink on a semi-slack line** and watch where the line enters the water. Many bites come on the fall. If the line jumps or swims sideways, a fish has it.
3. **Once it's on the bottom, raise the rod tip slowly** from about 9 o'clock to 11 o'clock to drag the worm a foot or two. For a hop, use a short, quicker lift.
4. **Lower the rod tip and reel up the slack**, then pause for a few seconds.
5. **Repeat** all the way back.

### What a bite feels like

Texas rig bites are often light. Watch and feel for:

- A sharp "tick" or tap, like someone flicked your line.
- The weight suddenly feeling mushy, spongy or heavier than it should.
- Your line moving off to one side.
- Slack line when you go to lift, because a fish picked it up and swam toward you.

### Set the hook hard

Don't swing on slack line. Point your rod tip toward the fish and reel until you feel weight. Then set hard with a quick, firm sweep of the rod up and over your shoulder. That force drives the point through the plastic. Keep the rod bent and keep reeling.

Before you swing, glance behind you. A missed hook set can fling a weighted hook back toward you, so keep kids and friends clear and wear sunglasses.

<Tip>When in doubt, set the hook. A miss costs you one cast. Waiting to be sure often gives the bass time to spit the worm.</Tip>

## Other baits to Texas rig

Any soft plastic with a solid head can be Texas rigged. Creature baits, with legs and flappers, are great for flipping into brush and docks.

## Common mistakes

- **A crooked worm.** It spins and twists your line. Re-rig any time it slides down the hook.
- **Too heavy a weight.** It falls too fast and snags more. Start light and go up only when you can't feel bottom.
- **Too small a hook.** The point can't get through the plastic, so you feel the bite and miss the fish.
- **Fishing too fast.** Drag, pause, repeat. Slow is the whole idea.
