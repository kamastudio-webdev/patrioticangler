To catch snapper and grouper, anchor or drift over a reef, wreck or rocky ledge and drop a circle hook baited with squid or cut fish on a knocker rig or a dropper loop rig. When a grouper bites, reel hard right away so it can't get back to the rocks. Keep a descending device ready for the fish you release.

## Where snapper and grouper live

Both live around hard structure on the bottom:

- **Natural reefs and hard bottom.** Patches of rock, coral and sponge surrounded by sand or mud.
- **Wrecks and artificial reefs.** Sunken ships, concrete rubble and reef modules. Many coastal states publish GPS numbers for their public artificial reefs.
- **Ledges.** Spots where the bottom drops a few feet suddenly, often with undercuts that grouper tuck into.

Snapper often school a few feet up off the structure. Grouper stay tight to the rocks and holes.

## Find fish with a depth finder

Over sand, a depth finder shows the bottom as a thin, smooth line. Hard bottom shows as a thicker, brighter line, and wrecks and reefs show as bumps and spikes. Fish appear as marks or arches above the structure.

When you find a good spot, save it on your GPS. Sit in neutral for a minute and watch which way the wind and current push the boat. Then motor up-current and drift back over the spot, or anchor up-current so the boat settles back over it as the anchor line comes tight.

## Gear for bottom fishing

Use a conventional reel on a 6- to 7-foot medium-heavy or heavy conventional rod. Spool it with 50-pound braid, which is thin enough to cut through current and lets you feel bites in deep water, then add a heavier leader for the rig.

## The knocker rig

On a knocker rig, an egg sinker slides on the leader and rests against the eye of the hook, so your bait sits tight to the bottom where grouper feed. When it snags, give slack and the sinker can slide down and knock the hook loose, which is where the name comes from.

To tie it:

1. Tie a barrel swivel to your main line.
2. Cut 3 to 4 feet of 40- to 60-pound fluorocarbon or mono leader. Use the heavier end for grouper.
3. Tie the leader to the other end of the swivel.
4. Slide an egg sinker onto the leader.
5. Tie a circle hook to the end. The sinker slides down and sits against the hook eye.

Use just enough weight to hold bottom, anywhere from 2 to 8 ounces or more depending on depth and current.

## The dropper loop, or chicken rig

The dropper loop rig, called a chicken rig on many boats, has the sinker at the bottom and one or two hooks on short loops above it. Your baits hang 1 to 3 feet off the bottom, right where schooling snapper swim.

Build it from 3 to 4 feet of heavy mono: tie a loop at the bottom for a bank sinker, tie one or two dropper loops above it about 18 inches apart, and put a circle hook on each loop. Coastal tackle shops also sell them already tied. Use the knocker rig for grouper and the chicken rig for snapper hovering above the bottom.

## Use circle hooks

As a fish swims off with the bait, the tightening line slides a circle hook to the corner of the jaw and sets it there. Fish are rarely gut-hooked, so the ones you release have a better chance of surviving.

Don't jerk the rod to set the hook. When the rod loads up, reel steadily and lift. As a starting point, use 4/0 to 6/0 circle hooks for most snapper, 7/0 to 9/0 for grouper, and 1/0 to 3/0 for smaller vermilion and mangrove snapper. Leave the hook point and gap uncovered when you bait up.

## Baits that work

- **Cut squid.** Tough, cheap and stays on the hook. Nearly everything on the reef eats it, including small fish you don't want, so check your bait often.
- **Sardines.** Whole for bigger fish or cut in half for snapper. They're soft, so hook them through the eyes.
- **Cigar minnows.** A firm, oily baitfish that both snapper and grouper love. Hook them through the eyes, or cut them into chunks.
- **Live pinfish.** A top grouper bait. Hook one through the lips or just behind the back fin and send it down on a knocker rig.

You can catch sardines and cigar minnows yourself with a sabiki rig, explained in [offshore fishing for beginners](/offshore-fishing-for-beginners/).

## Reel grouper up fast

A grouper bite often feels like a heavy thump, and then the fish turns and heads straight for its hole. If it gets into the rocks, it can wedge itself in or rub your line through. You have only a few seconds.

- Before you drop, set the drag tight, much tighter than you would for snapper.
- Brace the rod butt against your hip or in a rod belt, with the rod tip low.
- When the rod loads, crank hard and fast while lifting. Don't give the fish any line until it's well off the bottom.
- Once it's clear of the structure, pump it up: lift the rod, then reel as you lower it.

If a grouper does get into the rocks, put the reel in free spool for a minute. Sometimes the fish swims back out and you get another chance.

## How snapper bite

Snapper usually bite with quick taps and rattles as they pick at the bait. Don't jerk. With a circle hook, wait until the rod tip pulls down steadily, then reel and lift. If you keep losing baits to taps, try a smaller hook and a smaller piece of bait. Mangrove snapper are wary, so a lighter fluorocarbon leader can draw more bites.

Snapper have sharp teeth, so unhook them with long pliers instead of your fingers.

## Barotrauma and descending devices

When a fish is pulled up from deep water, the gas in its swim bladder expands. You may see bulging eyes, a stomach pushed out of the mouth, or a fish that floats and can't swim down. That's barotrauma. Left at the surface, the fish often dies or gets eaten.

A descending device sends the fish back down, where the pressure shrinks the gas again. A venting tool, a hollow needle that lets gas out of the swim bladder, also works but takes practice, so beginners do better with a descending device:

1. Remove the hook quickly. If the fish is hooked deep, cut the leader close to the hook.
2. Clip the descending device to the fish's lower lip.
3. Lower the fish headfirst over the side and let out line steadily.
4. Let the device carry the fish down and release it, ideally near the depth where it was caught.

## Know the rules before you go

Snapper and grouper are tightly regulated. Red snapper and several groupers have short seasons that change from year to year, plus size limits, bag limits and gear rules that differ between state and federal waters. Check NOAA Fisheries for federal waters and your state wildlife agency for state waters before every trip. You'll need a saltwater license, and in Florida you also need a free State Reef Fish Angler designation to fish for many reef fish from a private boat.

Gear rules to know in federal waters:

- **South Atlantic.** Since 2020, you must have a descending device on board and ready to use while fishing for or possessing snapper-grouper species. The device needs at least a 16-ounce weight and at least 60 feet of line. North of 28 degrees north latitude, which crosses Florida's Atlantic coast in Brevard County, you must use non-offset, non-stainless steel circle hooks when fishing for snapper-grouper with natural bait. South of that line, hooks used with natural bait must be non-stainless steel.
- **Gulf.** Non-stainless steel circle hooks are required when fishing for reef fish with natural bait, and you need a dehooking device on board. Starting January 13, 2022, the DESCEND Act also required a descending device or venting tool rigged and ready while fishing for reef fish. That federal requirement expired in January 2026. The Gulf Council has voted to make it permanent, and NOAA Fisheries is working on a rule to bring it back, so check the current status before your trip and carry one either way.
