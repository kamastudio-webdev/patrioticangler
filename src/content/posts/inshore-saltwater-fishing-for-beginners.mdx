To start inshore saltwater fishing, spool a 7-foot medium spinning rod with 10-pound braid, add 2 to 3 feet of 20-pound fluorocarbon leader, and fish a live shrimp under a popping cork or a soft plastic on a 1/4-ounce jig head. Go during the first few hours of a moving tide and cast around oyster bars, grass flats and docks. That one setup catches redfish, speckled trout and flounder along the Gulf and Southeast coasts.

## What inshore means

Inshore water is the shallow, sheltered saltwater close to land: bays, marshes, tidal creeks, mangrove shorelines and the areas around inlets. Much of it is 1 to 6 feet deep. You don't need a boat: piers, bridges, seawalls and wadeable flats all hold fish, and a kayak or small boat just reaches more spots.

## Fish you'll catch

- **Redfish (red drum).** Copper-bronze with one or more black spots near the tail. They root along the bottom for crabs and shrimp, sometimes in water so shallow their backs stick out. They pull hard and aren't picky.
- **Speckled trout (spotted seatrout).** Silver sides with black spots on the back and tail. Their mouths are soft and tear easily, so set your drag a little lighter and don't horse them in.
- **Flounder.** A flat fish that lies on the bottom with both eyes on one side and ambushes bait. Southern and Gulf flounder live in the South, and summer flounder (fluke) farther north. Fish slowly and right on the bottom near drop-offs, docks and creek mouths.
- **Snook (Florida).** Mostly a central and south Florida fish, found around mangroves, docks, bridges and inlets. The edge of a snook's gill cover is sharp enough to cut line and fingers, so keep your hands away from it.

### A short note for the Northeast

From the Chesapeake Bay up to Maine, the main inshore fish are striped bass and bluefish. The same ideas work there: fish moving water near structure and baitfish. Bluefish have teeth that slice through fluorocarbon, so use a short wire leader when they're around and unhook them with pliers. Striper rules change often and include gear rules, such as circle hooks when fishing with bait, so read your state's current regulations before you go.

## Fish the moving tide

Tides matter more than anything else inshore. Moving water carries shrimp, crabs and small fish, and redfish and trout set up where the current brings food to them. When the tide goes slack and the water stops, the bite usually slows.

The simple rule: fish the first 2 to 3 hours after the tide starts moving, whether it's coming in or going out.

- **Incoming (rising) tide.** Water pushes in from the ocean, and fish follow it up onto flats, into the marsh grass and along shorelines.
- **Outgoing (falling) tide.** Water drains off the flats and out of the creeks, carrying bait with it. Fish wait at creek mouths, drains and the edges of oyster bars to eat what washes out.

Check a tide app or chart for the station closest to where you'll fish. Tides run about 50 minutes later each day, so a good morning tide this week turns into an afternoon tide next week. In summer, a moving tide at dawn or dusk is ideal, since shallow water heats up by midday.

<Tip>Cast up-current and let your bait drift with the water toward the structure. A shrimp swept along by the tide looks natural, and fish face into the current waiting for exactly that.</Tip>

## Find the structure

Inshore fish hold near anything that breaks the current or holds food:

- **Oyster bars.** Clumps of oysters hold crabs and shrimp, and reds and trout cruise the edges. The shells cut line and skin, so wear shoes if you wade and keep your boat or kayak off them.
- **Grass flats.** Seagrass beds in 2 to 5 feet of water are classic speckled trout water. Look for sandy potholes in the grass, where redfish often sit.
- **Docks.** Pilings give shade and grow barnacles that draw bait. Snook, reds and flounder hold close to them. Respect private property.
- **Mangroves.** In Florida, mangrove roots along the shore hold snook and redfish, especially on high tide.
- **Jetties.** Rock walls at inlets have strong current along their sides that draws reds, flounder, stripers and bluefish. Stay off the lower, wet rocks, which are slick.
- **Creek mouths.** Where a tidal creek empties into a bay, a falling tide pulls bait right past waiting fish.

## One spinning setup does it all

One setup covers redfish, trout, flounder and small snook:

- **Rod:** 7-foot, medium power spinning rod
- **Reel:** 2500 to 3000 size spinning reel
- **Main line:** 10- to 15-pound braid
- **Leader:** 2 to 3 feet of 20-pound fluorocarbon

### Rinse your gear after every trip

Salt eats reels, rod guides and hooks. After every trip, even a short one:

1. Tighten the reel's drag so water doesn't seep into it.
2. Spray the rod and reel with a gentle shower of fresh water from a hose. Skip high pressure, which drives salt into the reel.
3. Shake off the water and let everything air-dry out of direct sun.
4. Loosen the drag before you put the reel away.

Rinse pliers and tackle trays too, and toss rusty hooks. Corrosion-resistant saltwater pliers are worth having for unhooking fish and cutting braid, since ordinary steel pliers rust fast.

## Three baits that catch fish

### Live shrimp under a popping cork

This is the most reliable way for a beginner to catch speckled trout and redfish. Buy live shrimp at a bait shop and keep them in an aerated bucket. Hook one crosswise through the hard horn on top of its head, avoiding the dark spot inside the head, and hang it under a popping cork. Pop the cork, let it sit, and watch for it to go under. The guide on [how to fish a popping cork](/how-to-fish-a-popping-cork/) covers the rig and the rhythm step by step.

### Soft plastic on a jig head

A 3- to 4-inch paddle tail or a soft plastic shrimp on a 1/4-ounce jig head catches all of these fish and lets you cover water fast. Cast, let it sink to the bottom, then reel slowly with an occasional lift of the rod tip so it hops and swims just above the bottom. Switch to a 1/8-ounce head in very shallow water and a 3/8-ounce head in deeper water or strong current.

### Cut bait for redfish

Redfish find food by smell, so a chunk of fresh mullet or menhaden (also called pogy or bunker), or a piece of blue crab, sitting on the bottom is hard for them to pass up. Use a chunk about 1 inch across on a 3/0 or 4/0 circle hook. Rig it with a 1/2- to 1-ounce egg sinker above a swivel, the same sliding rig used in [catfish fishing](/catfish-fishing-for-beginners/). Cast it to a creek mouth or the edge of an oyster bar and set the rod in a holder. When it bends and stays bent, reel steadily instead of jerking.

## License, rules and safety

Saltwater fishing usually needs a saltwater license, separate from a freshwater one. Redfish, trout, flounder, snook and stripers all have size and bag limits, and some have slot limits or closed seasons that change from year to year, so check your state wildlife agency before you keep anything. In Florida, you also need a snook permit on top of your saltwater license to keep a snook.

A few safety basics:

- Wear a life jacket in a kayak or small boat, and keep one on kids anytime they're near the water.
- When wading, shuffle your feet along the bottom instead of taking steps. It warns stingrays to move out of the way.
- Bring a hat, polarized sunglasses and plenty of water. Sun bouncing off shallow flats burns fast.
